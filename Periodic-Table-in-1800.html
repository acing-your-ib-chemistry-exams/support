The 25 elements known outright by 1800

Nine of them — gold, silver, copper, iron, tin, lead, mercury, carbon and sulfur — had been known and worked since antiquity, long before anyone had a concept of “element” in the modern sense. Arsenic and antimony followed in the medieval and early-modern periods, recognisable as distinct substances well before formal chemistry existed. The remaining four ancient-adjacent metals were pinned down across the 18th century by named individuals: cobalt (Georg Brandt, 1735), platinum (recognised by European chemists from the 1730s–40s onward, though rendered reliably malleable only in 1786 by Chabaneau), nickel (Axel Cronstedt, 1751) and zinc (isolated in Europe by Andreas Marggraf in 1746, though metallic zinc had been produced in India and China centuries earlier).

Then came a run of gas- and mineral-chemistry discoveries in the 1760s–90s: hydrogen (Cavendish, 1766), manganese (Johan Gottlieb Gahn, 1774), nitrogen (Daniel Rutherford, 1772), oxygen (Priestley, 1774), phosphorus (Hennig Brand, 1669 — the odd one out, isolated a century before the rest of this group), molybdenum (Peter Jacob Hjelm, 1781), tellurium (Franz-Joseph Müller von Reichenstein, 1782, named by Klaproth 1798), tungsten (the Elhuyar brothers, 1783), chromium (Louis-Nicolas Vauquelin, 1797) and bismuth (established as distinct from lead by Claude François Geoffroy in 1753).

The 8 half-known “earths” and gases (marked with *)

  • Chlorine (1774). Scheele made the gas but believed it was a compound (“dephlogisticated marine acid”); Davy didn't prove it elemental until 1810.
  • Beryllium (1798). Vauquelin identified its oxide as distinct from aluminium's; the metal itself wasn't isolated until 1828.
  • Barium (1774). Scheele identified baryta as a distinct earth; Davy isolated the metal by electrolysis in 1808.
  • Strontium (1790). Adair Crawford spotted strontia as chemically distinct from baryta and lime; Davy isolated the metal in 1808.
  • Yttrium (1794). Johan Gadolin found yttria, the first of the rare-earth “earths,” in a quarry near Ytterby, Sweden; an impure metal followed only in 1828.
  • Zirconium (1789) and Titanium (1791). Klaproth found zirconia in zircon in 1789; William Gregor (and, independently, Klaproth) found titanium's oxide in 1791. Neither metal was isolated until well into the 19th or even 20th century (titanium metal not produced pure until 1910).
  • Uranium (1789). Klaproth found a new, dense “earth” in pitchblende ore and named it after the newly discovered planet Uranus. The metal itself wasn't isolated until 1841 (Eugène-Melchior Péligot) — and its radioactivity, of course, was unimaginable in 1800; that was still ninety-six years away.

Everything that just missed the cut

Here's the thing that's easy to miss: Volta's pile wasn't lying around waiting to be discovered in 1800 by coincidence — it was announced in March 1800, and it's the reason everything after it moves so fast. Before the pile, chemists could only attack a stubborn “earth” with heat, carbon, or another element already known to be more reactive — and for things like soda, potash, lime and baryta, nothing available was strong enough to crack them open. Davy's whole 1807–08 run (Na, K, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba) depends on having a battery large enough to force electrolysis through molten or dissolved salts. So 1800 isn't an arbitrary “before” line that happens to sit awkwardly close to a cluster of discoveries — it's closer to a hinge date: the year the tool that unlocks the next thirty years shows up, just before anyone's had a chance to use it on these particular elements.

But electricity is only half the story. The other engine running alongside it is mineral analysis — and it's driven by two things arriving together: better technique, and a lot more ore to point it at. Klaproth in Berlin and Vauquelin in Paris were making quantitative, gravimetric analysis genuinely rigorous for the first time — weighing precipitates precisely enough to prove a mineral really did contain something new, rather than an already-known substance in an unfamiliar form. At the same time, mining, trade and colonial extraction were putting a much wider range of raw ore in front of European chemists than ever before: a heavy Mexican lead ore that turned out to hide vanadium (Andrés Manuel del Río, 1801); an obscure black mineral called columbite, sent to the British Museum from Connecticut, that hid niobium (Charles Hatchett, 1801); Swedish minerals that hid tantalum (Anders Ekeberg, 1802) and, a year later, cerium — found independently by Berzelius and Hisinger in Sweden and by Klaproth in Berlin, all three working from the same mineral, cerite. And crude South American platinum ore, arriving in Europe in growing quantities, turned out to be not one metal but at least five: William Hyde Wollaston and Smithson Tennant spent 1802–04 picking palladium, rhodium, osmium and iridium out of the very same crucibles.

So the honest answer to “why did so much happen right around 1800?” isn't luck. It's that two separate capabilities — a battery strong enough to force reluctant compounds apart, and an analytical method rigorous enough to prove a genuinely new substance was hiding in a familiar-looking rock — both matured in the same few years, and were immediately turned loose on a backlog of “earths” and ores that had been sitting there, unresolved, for decades.

What's missing altogether

Every noble gas (helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon) is absent, because none of them react chemically — there was no reaction in 1800 that could have revealed one. They needed 19th-century spectroscopy and precision gas-density measurement, tools that simply didn't exist yet: argon came in 1894, helium (on Earth) in 1895, neon and krypton and xenon all in 1898. The halogens beyond chlorine (fluorine, bromine, iodine), the alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs), most of the second and third rows of transition metals, and every lanthanide are absent for the same underlying reason as sodium and potassium: they were bound up in compounds too stable for 18th-century chemistry to crack, waiting for electrolysis, better furnaces, or (in the rare-earths' case) another century of increasingly fine mineral separation.

Click through to 1830 — and where Germany comes in

Press the button above the table and twenty more elements light up red. Eight of the nineteen brand-new ones and six of the eight 1800-era “earths” get sorted out in a single, frantic three-decade stretch: vanadium and niobium in 1801, tantalum in 1802, then a rush of five metals — cerium, palladium, rhodium, osmium, iridium — in 1803–04 alone, several of them teased out of the very same crate of crude South American platinum ore that William Hyde Wollaston and Smithson Tennant were dissecting in London. Humphry Davy then spent 1807–08 taking an electric pile to every stubborn “earth” in the cabinet and walked away with sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium, one after another. Iodine followed in 1811, lithium, cadmium and selenium in 1817, silicon in 1824, aluminium (impure) in 1825, and bromine in 1826. Thorium turns up too, but only in its half-known, dashed-red form — Berzelius found its “earth” in 1828, and nobody would isolate the metal for decades yet.

Three of these have a gold ring, because they were German finds, made by chemists working in exactly the university and state-laboratory culture this course keeps returning to. Cerium (1803) was found independently and almost simultaneously by Berzelius and Hisinger in Sweden and by Martin Heinrich Klaproth in Berlin — the same Klaproth who had already found uranium, zirconium and titanium's “earths” before 1800. Cadmium (1817) was isolated by Friedrich Stromeyer, a chemistry professor at Göttingen, while investigating why a batch of zinc carbonate looked slightly off-colour. And beryllium metal (1828) was produced independently, within weeks of each other, by Friedrich Wöhler in Germany and Antoine Bussy in France — the same Wöhler who, the year before, had also produced Europe's first reasonably pure sample of metallic aluminium. None of this is a coincidence: German universities were building exactly the kind of well-equipped teaching and research laboratories in this period that Liebig would later make famous at Gießen, and mineral analysis — finding new “earths” in ores from Saxony, Bohemia and further afield — was one of the things those laboratories were best at.
