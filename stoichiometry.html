Grade 10 Industrial Chemistry

Course materials for the Grade 10 Industrial Chemistry course

Course Overview — the five units, from the blast furnace to the ammonia plant
iThe full course overview for Grade 10 Industrial Chemistry, with the weekly timetable embedded at the top. Unit 1 reviews the four tools you need all year — equations and moles, thermochemistry, kinetics and equilibrium. Unit 2 takes you through the Industrial Revolution and the railway explosion: metallic and ionic bonding, alloys, the reactivity series, the blast furnace, aluminium from bauxite, corrosion, redox, and a first look at reactors and process flow diagrams. Unit 3 builds acid–base theory from Arrhenius to Brønsted–Lowry, with titration, pH probes and LoggerPro, ammonia and the transition-metal complexes. Unit 4 runs from Newlands’ octaves of 1866 through Mendeleev to electron configuration, Aufbau and periodicity. Unit 5 puts the whole lot to work on Haber–Bosch and the Contact process, and on the chemical corridor of the Rhine and Main. Every section links straight through to the matching resources on Canvas.
The five units of the course set out in full — what each one covers, how they build on one another, and links to every resource on Canvas. With the weekly timetable embedded at the top.
Part 1: How industrialised was Germany in 1800?
iThe chemical trades of the Rhine and the Main, from the old Empire to the first railway (1775 – September 1825). Before asking how the great chemical works of the Rhine–Main valley came to be, this background reading looks at what was actually there before them — and the answer is not the one the phrase “industrial revolution” leads you to expect.
Background reading for Unit 1: what chemical trades and industry actually existed in the Rhine–Main region before 1800, setting up Part 2's story of German industrialisation.
Part 2: The Development of the German Chemical Industries, 1830–1871
iHow industrialised was Germany becoming? From the Zollverein customs union of 1834 and the railway reaching Höchst in 1839, through Liebig's teaching laboratory at Gießen, the chemistry of the gasworks that gave the world coal-tar dyes, and Kekulé's benzene ring, to the founding of Höchst, Bayer, BASF and Cassella and the proclamation of the German Empire in 1871. Illustrated with primary German sources (with translations), historical maps and photographs.
From the customs union to the Empire — the forty-one years that built the Rhine–Main chemical industry, with primary sources, maps and photographs throughout.
Part 3: Iron, Empire, and the Nitrogen War, 1871–1918
iFrom the founding of the German Empire in 1871 to the armistice of 11 November 1918. The Gilchrist–Thomas process turns phosphoric iron ore into steel and reshapes the Ruhr; the Alizarine Convention and the rise of the dye giants; Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch fix nitrogen from the air, and that same process keeps Germany's guns firing through a war that Chilean saltpetre could no longer reach by sea. Illustrated with primary German sources (with translations), historical maps and photographs, and local Taunus history throughout.
From the Empire's founding to the armistice — steel, dyes, and the Haber–Bosch process that fed Germany's guns once the British blockade cut off Chilean nitrates.
Part 4: Bread, Gold, and a Merger of Giants, 1918–1933
iFrom the armistice to Hitler's appointment as Chancellor on 30 January 1933. Reparations and hyperinflation; Fritz Haber's Nobel Prize controversy and his failed attempt to extract gold from seawater to help pay war debts; Friedrich Bergius and Carl Bosch's high-pressure coal-to-oil process; the 2 December 1925 merger that created IG Farben, the "Council of Gods"; and the quiet collapse and rebirth of Motorenfabrik Oberursel in the Taunus hills. The reading ends deliberately at the hinge of 1933, before the story it sets up in Part 5.
Reparations, Haber's gold-from-seawater scheme, the Bergius process, and the 1925 merger that created IG Farben — ending flatly at Hitler's appointment as Chancellor.
Part 5: IG Farben in the Third Reich, 1933–1945
iPart 4 ended, deliberately flatly, on 30 January 1933 — IG Farben was the largest chemical company on Earth, its pre-1933 management contained no Nazi supporters, and its supervisory board included prominent Jewish industrialists. This reading follows the same company through the next twelve years: how it was captured by a regime it had not supported, built a synthetic-rubber factory beside a death camp, and answered for it — only partly — in a Nuremberg courtroom.
How the largest chemical company on Earth was captured by the Nazi regime, built a factory beside a death camp, and answered for it — only partly — at Nuremberg.
Part 6: Two Chemistries, One Country, 1951–1990
iPart 5 ended in 1951–52, with IG Farben formally broken back into BASF, Bayer, Hoechst and smaller successors including Agfa. This reading follows what those pieces, and the sites this series has already visited — Ludwigshafen, Leverkusen, Frankfurt, Leuna, the Buna works, and Motorenfabrik Oberursel in the Taunus — did with the next thirty-nine years: really two stories running side by side either side of one internal German border, until the border itself came down.
The same firms, sites and chemistry, rebuilt as rival economic systems either side of the German border — from IG Farben's 1951 break-up to reunification.
Part 7: One Company Dissolves, Another Cannot Afford the Gas, 1990–Present
iThe last reading in the series. Part 6 ended on 3 October 1990, deliberately without following Leuna, Buna or the rest of the former East German chemical industry into what reunification actually did to them. This reading picks that up, and carries the whole series — Ludwigshafen, Leverkusen, Frankfurt, Leuna, the Buna works, and Motorenfabrik Oberursel — all the way to the present day, closing a story that began, in Part 1, with a scattering of small chemical trades along the Rhine.
Reunification's economics, the giants IG Farben's successors became, and why the company this series began with is, in 2026, struggling to afford its own gas bill.
