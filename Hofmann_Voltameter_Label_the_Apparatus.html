What is happening here?

A Hofmann voltameter is used to electrolyse water into its two elements. A small amount of acid or alkali is added to the water so that it conducts electricity (pure water alone is too poor a conductor). When a direct current is passed through, water molecules are split apart at each electrode:

  • At the anode (connected to the positive terminal), water is oxidised and oxygen gas is given off.
  • At the cathode (connected to the negative terminal), water is reduced and hydrogen gas is given off.

Because water is H2O, electrolysis produces hydrogen and oxygen in a 2:1 ratio by volume — which is exactly what the diagram shows: the tube on the right collects twice the volume of gas as the tube on the left. The gas bubbles rise from each electrode and collect at the top of their tube, pushing the liquid level down; the connecting tube along the bottom keeps both sides filled with the same electrolyte and lets ions move between the two half-cells to complete the circuit.

Electrolysis
Using an electric current to break down a compound into its elements.
Electrolyte
The liquid (here, water with a little acid or alkali added) that conducts electricity by allowing ions to move through it.
Electrode
A conductor (often an unreactive metal such as platinum) that carries current into or out of the electrolyte.
Anode
The electrode connected to the positive terminal of the power source. Negative ions move towards it and are oxidised (lose electrons).
Cathode
The electrode connected to the negative terminal of the power source. Positive ions move towards it and are reduced (gain electrons).
