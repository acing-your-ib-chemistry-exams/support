Glossary — quick reference

Exothermic
A reaction that releases energy to the surroundings. The products end up with less energy than the reactants, and ΔH is negative.
Endothermic
A reaction that absorbs energy from the surroundings. The products end up with more energy than the reactants, and ΔH is positive.
ΔH (enthalpy change)
The energy difference between products and reactants, measured in kJ/mol. Negative for exothermic reactions, positive for endothermic reactions.
Activation energy (Ea)
The minimum energy needed before a reaction can happen — the height of the energy barrier from the reactants level up to the peak of the curve.
Limiting reactant
Whichever reactant runs out first, stopping the reaction. Everything else (here, the acid) is in excess.
Specific heat capacity
The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1°C. For water, this is 4.2 J/g/°C.

A. Level Diagrams for Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions (Ignoring Activation Energy)

Reading these diagrams Each diagram plots energy (vertical axis) against reaction progress (horizontal axis), with no activation-energy hump — just a reactants level and a products level. If the products level is lower than the reactants level, energy has been given out to the surroundings: the reaction is exothermic, and ΔH is negative. If the products level is higher, energy has been taken in from the surroundings: the reaction is endothermic, and ΔH is positive.

B. Level Diagrams Including Activation Energy

Adding activation energy Every reaction — exothermic or endothermic — has to climb over an energy barrier first, called the activation energy, Ea. On the diagram this is drawn as a hump: the peak is always the highest point on the curve, higher than both the reactants and the products. Ea is measured from the reactants level up to the peak. ΔH is still measured from the reactants level to the products level, exactly as before — the hump doesn't change ΔH at all.

D. Calorimetry: Four Common Reactions

Cheat sheet — Calorimetry with Water

The formula
thermal energy change (q) = volume of solution reacting × 4.2 × temperature change. This treats the solution as if it were pure water (1 mL ≈ 1 g), with a specific heat capacity of 4.2 J per gram per °C.
Finding energy per mole of the limiting reactant
1. Calculate q from the formula above. 2. Divide q by the mass of the limiting reactant to get energy per gram. 3. Use the limiting reactant's molar mass to convert moles, then divide q by moles instead (or multiply your per-gram answer by the molar mass) to get energy per mole.
Why the acid must be in excess
If the acid is in excess (XS), the metal is the limiting reactant — it all dissolves. That's exactly what lets you calculate moles of metal reacted directly from the mass you started with, with nothing left over and unreacted.
Fresh worked example 0.20 g of magnesium ribbon is added to 40 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid (in excess). All of the magnesium dissolves, and the temperature rises by 16.7 °C.
  1. q = volume × 4.2 × temperature change = 40 × 4.2 × 16.7 = 2805.6 J
  2. Energy per gram of magnesium = 2805.6 ÷ 0.20 = 14028 J/g
  3. Moles of magnesium = 0.20 ÷ 24 (molar mass of Mg) = 0.00833 mol
  4. Energy per mole = 2805.6 ÷ 0.00833 = 336700 J/mol ≈ 336.7 kJ/mol
Since the reaction is exothermic (the temperature rose), this is quoted as ΔH = −336.7 kJ/mol.

Idea D3 — A second exothermic reaction: neutralisation

1. 25 cm³ of 2.0 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid is mixed with an equal volume of 2.0 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide, which exactly neutralises it (HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O). The temperature of the mixture rises by 13.2°C. Calculate the energy released per mole of water formed, in kJ/mol (to 1 decimal place).

HintMoles of HCl (and of NaOH, and of water formed) = 25 ÷ 1000 × 2.0. The total volume heated is both solutions added together, 50 cm³, since they're mixed into one beaker. q = total volume × 4.2 × 13.2. Energy per mole = q ÷ moles of water formed, then convert J/mol to kJ/mol.
Model answer (advanced)Moles of water formed = 25 ÷ 1000 × 2.0 = 0.05 mol. q = 50 × 4.2 × 13.2 = 2772 J. Energy per mole = 2772 ÷ 0.05 = 55440 J/mol = 55.4 kJ/mol. Idea D4 — An endothermic example: dissolving ammonium nitrate

1. 4.0 g of solid ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (molar mass = 80 g/mol), is added to 50 cm³ of water in an insulated cup and stirred until it all dissolves. The temperature of the water falls by 6.1°C. Calculate the energy absorbed per mole of ammonium nitrate, in kJ/mol (to 1 decimal place).

HintMoles of NH₄NO₃ = 4.0 ÷ 80. q = 50 × 4.2 × 6.1 (use the size of the temperature fall, not a negative number). Energy per mole = q ÷ moles, then convert J/mol to kJ/mol. The water is losing this energy to the dissolving process, which is why it cools down.
Model answer (advanced)Moles of NH₄NO₃ = 4.0 ÷ 80 = 0.05 mol. q = 50 × 4.2 × 6.1 = 1281 J. Energy per mole = 1281 ÷ 0.05 = 25620 J/mol = 25.6 kJ/mol. Since the water cooled down, the dissolving process absorbed energy from it — this is endothermic, quoted as ΔH = +25.6 kJ/mol, close to the accepted value of about +25.7 kJ/mol. This is the same process used in instant cold packs.
