The Periodic Table in 1871

A second companion, picked up where The Periodic Table in 1830 leaves off: this is the table as it stood at the close of Part 2, sixty-two of the ninety-two elements known or half-known, spectroscopy just three years old and already reshaping how new elements got found at all. The 59 elements known outright by 1871

This baseline is exactly where The Periodic Table in 1830 leaves off, toggled forward: fifty of these were already in hand by 1830, and see that page (and The Periodic Table in 1800 behind it) for the full story of how they arrived. The nine new arrivals in between are the ones worth re-reading here, because between them they mark a hinge in the whole discipline. Lanthanum, terbium and erbium (1839–43) came from Carl Gustaf Mosander in Stockholm patiently proving that earlier “single” rare-earth minerals were themselves mixtures — the first hint of just how crowded that corner of the table would turn out to be. Uranium finally got a metal to go with its 1789 “earth” in 1841, and ruthenium, the last of the six platinum-group metals, turned up in 1844 in ore that London chemists had already picked over twice and discarded. Then, in 1859–60, Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff at Heidelberg built a spectroscope, and caesium and rubidium (1860–61) became the first two elements ever found by an optical fingerprint rather than a chemical reaction; thallium and indium followed within three years, proof the method wasn't a one-off. It is that same method, matured and pointed at fainter and fainter signals, that accounts for almost everything new between here and 1918.

The 3 elements still only half-known by 1871 (marked with *)

  • Titanium (1791). Detected as an oxide by William Gregor and, independently, Klaproth; a truly pure sample of the metal wouldn't exist until 1910. Watch this tile when you click through to 1918.
  • Thorium (1828). Berzelius found its “earth” in a Norwegian mineral; like titanium, the pure metal resisted every attempt for decades, and this table's cut-off arrives before it's finally cracked.
  • Helium (1868). The newest and strangest of the three — not a mineral at all, but a single unexplained yellow line in the Sun's spectrum, recorded during a total eclipse by the French astronomer Pierre Jules César Janssen and confirmed soon after by Norman Lockyer and Edward Frankland in London, who gave it its solar name. In 1871 nobody had so much as weighed a sample of it.

What's still missing by 1871

Every noble gas but helium's ghostly solar trace is absent, because none of them react chemically and nothing in an 1871 laboratory could catch a gas that does nothing at all. Most of the rare-earth family is still bound up together in a handful of minerals from Ytterby and Bastnäs, indistinguishable from one another by any test yet available. And nothing radioactive exists on this table at all — radioactivity itself hasn't been discovered yet, so polonium, radium, actinium, radon and protactinium aren't just unfound, they're unimaginable: no one yet has a reason to go looking for an element that gives itself away by falling apart.

Click through to 1918 — three routes to twenty-three new elements

Press the button above the table and the biggest single wave of discovery in this table's history lights up red, arriving down three quite different roads. The first is spectroscopy, matured. François Lecoq de Boisbaudran in Paris used it to catch gallium (1875) and samarium (1879) — gallium a spectacular confirmation of Mendeleev's periodic table, which had predicted an “eka-aluminium” with almost exactly gallium's properties years before anyone found it. Lars Fredrik Nilson in Uppsala found scandium (1879), the periodic table's other predicted gap, in the same way. And a succession of chemists — Jean Charles Galissard de Marignac in Geneva, Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran again, Carl Auer von Welsbach in Vienna, Georges Urbain in Paris — spent the 1878–1907 window splitting the rare earths apart still further: ytterbium, holmium, thulium, gadolinium, praseodymium, neodymium, dysprosium, europium and finally lutetium, the last stable element on the table, isolated by Urbain in 1907 (Auer von Welsbach isolated the same element independently at almost the same time and called it “cassiopeium” — Urbain's name and priority claim eventually won out, though the dispute ran for years). Germanium (1886) belongs with this group too: Clemens Winkler at the Freiberg mining academy in Saxony found it in a rare silver ore and realized, comparing its properties to Mendeleev's table, that he'd filled in “eka-silicon,” the periodic law's third and most exact prediction in a row.

The second road is physical, not chemical: liquefy the air itself and see what's left. In 1894 Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay in London found that nitrogen extracted from air was consistently, mysteriously denser than nitrogen made from chemical compounds — the residue was argon, a gas that reacted with nothing at all, and the first confirmed member of an entirely new column of the table. Ramsay and Morris Travers spent early 1898 fractionally distilling liquid air to find the rest of argon's family in a single frantic few weeks: krypton, neon and xenon, all three previously invisible because, like argon, they do nothing a 19th-century chemist knew how to look for. Fluorine (1886), the last of the halogens to fall, took a very different kind of stubbornness: Henri Moissan in Paris finally isolated it by electrolysis after decades of failed, sometimes fatal attempts by other chemists — it is savagely reactive with almost everything, including the equipment used to contain it.

The third road didn't exist before 1896. Once Henri Becquerel discovered that uranium ore gave off its own penetrating rays, Marie and Pierre Curie in Paris spent 1898 tracking that radioactivity through tonnes of pitchblende residue to two new elements far too scarce to detect any other way: polonium, named for Marie Curie's native Poland, and radium, whose faint blue glow made it instantly famous. Radioactivity kept paying out new elements for the next twenty years. André-Louis Debierne, working in the Curies' own Paris laboratory, identified actinium in 1899 (Friedrich Oskar Giesel in Germany found the same element independently soon after, and the priority dispute between them was never fully settled). Friedrich Ernst Dorn in Halle found radon in 1900, a radioactive gas given off by radium itself — the only element on this table both a noble gas and a product of decay, and the only new noble gas with a German name attached to it. And the last element on this table, protactinium, came from Otto Hahn and Lise Meitner at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute in Berlin in January 1917 (Frederick Soddy and John Cranston in Glasgow reached the same element by a different radioactive decay chain at almost exactly the same time). It was one small, early result from a partnership that would go on to matter a great deal more to twentieth-century physics — a story for another day.
