The Filtration Bench

Your class just prepared and filtered a precipitate by hand. These rounds keep the vocabulary, the apparatus, and the chemistry straight in your head before next week's class, when you'll examine your dried product.

Sequence

Put the preparation back in order

Read this first — it tells you what actually happened at the bench, and the order below follows from it, not from guesswork.

Two students, A and B, share a bench. Before anything is opened, both put on eye protection — sodium hydroxide and sodium carbonate are corrosive, and sodium hydroxide in the eyes causes blindness. Once that's on, Student A takes a 40 mL beaker and measures out 20 mL of magnesium sulfate solution. While A is doing that, Student B takes a second beaker and measures out 20 mL of sodium carbonate solution — carefully, because it's corrosive.

With both solutions ready, Student B moves on to the filter: first, B collects a circle of filter paper and weighs it on the balance before touching it further, writing the mass on the paper in pencil along with their name and the time. Only after the mass is recorded does B fold that same circle — in half, then half again, then half again — into a cone. B then fetches a 250 mL conical flask, stands a plastic funnel in it, and sets the folded paper cone inside the funnel.

Now the two solutions are combined: the mixture is poured, with care, through the filter funnel. As it drips through, the clear liquid — the filtrate — collects in the flask, while the solid left on the paper — the residue — is what the two students actually came for. A little distilled water is poured through afterwards to rinse the byproduct (sodium sulfate) off the residue, so it isn't carried through to the final product. Finally, once dripping has stopped, the paper is lifted out and spread flat to dry completely before next week.

Why weigh the dry filter paper before you've even filtered anything?

Next week, once your paper and its residue have dried completely, you'll weigh the paper again — now with the product sitting on it. Subtract the mass you wrote down today (the paper alone) from next week's mass (paper + dried product), and what's left is the mass of pure product you actually made. Skip today's weighing, and next week's number means nothing on its own.
