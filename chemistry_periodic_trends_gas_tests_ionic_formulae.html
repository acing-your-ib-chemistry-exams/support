Four practical, observation-based topics: the trends you've seen across Group I and Group 0, the four gas tests you've carried out in the lab, explaining why ionic compounds have the formulae they do in terms of ion charges, and predicting the precipitate formed from carbonate and hydroxide reactions. Sections A, B and D are self-marking with hints; Section C is for you to write your own explanations, with a model answer to check against.

Glossary

Group
A vertical column of the periodic table. Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons and similar chemical properties.
Alkali metals (Group I)
Lithium, sodium, potassium and the elements below them — soft, reactive metals that react vigorously with water.
Noble gases (Group 0)
Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon — unreactive gases at the right-hand edge of the periodic table.
Ion
An atom (or group of atoms) that has lost or gained electrons, giving it an overall electric charge.
Ionic formula
Written so that the total positive charge exactly cancels the total negative charge, using the smallest whole-number ratio of ions.

A. Periodic Trends You Have Seen

Two trends you've observed directly in class: how Group I (the alkali metals) behave as you go down the group, and how the noble gases in Group 0 change as you go down that group.

What you've seen — Group I Reactivity with water increases going down Group I: lithium reacts steadily, sodium more vigorously, and potassium vigorously enough to ignite. The metals also get softer going down the group — easier to cut with a knife, with a duller shine that tarnishes faster in air.
What you've seen — Group 0 The noble gases get denser going down the group, because each one has a greater molar mass than the one above it.

1. Going down Group I (the alkali metals), how does reactivity with water change?

HintCompare how vigorously lithium, sodium and potassium each react with water.
Model answer (advanced)Reactivity increases going down the group — potassium reacts far more vigorously than lithium.

2. Which would be easier to cut with a knife: sodium or potassium?

HintSoftness increases going down Group I, the same direction as reactivity.
Model answer (advanced)Potassium — it is softer than sodium, which is itself softer than lithium.

3. Which noble gas is the least dense: helium or radon?

HintDensity increases going down Group 0, so compare their positions in the group.
Model answer (advanced)Helium, right at the top of Group 0, is the least dense noble gas; radon, right at the bottom, is by far the densest.

4. Going down Group 0 (the noble gases), how does density change: does it increase, decrease, or stay the same?

HintCompare the molar mass of helium (4 g/mol) with the molar mass of radon (222 g/mol).
Model answer (advanced)It increases — each noble gas going down the group has a greater molar mass than the one above it, and a greater molar mass (in the same volume of gas) means a greater density.
Worth checking before you answer It's tempting to assume every noble gas balloon floats like helium does — but that's not true. A balloon floats only if the gas inside is less dense than air (average molar mass ≈ 29 g/mol). Compare each noble gas's molar mass with 29 before deciding.

5. True or false: a balloon filled with argon gas (molar mass 40 g/mol) floats in air, in the same way a helium balloon does?

HintCompare argon's molar mass (40 g/mol) with air's average molar mass (about 29 g/mol). Is argon heavier or lighter?
Model answer (advanced)False. Argon's molar mass, 40 g/mol, is greater than air's average of about 29 g/mol, so an argon-filled balloon actually sinks, not floats — unlike helium (4 g/mol) and neon (20 g/mol), which are both genuinely lighter than air.

6. Which two noble gases are light enough to make a balloon float, because their molar mass is less than air's (about 29 g/mol)?

HintCheck the molar masses: He = 4, Ne = 20, Ar = 40, Kr = 84, Xe = 131, Rn = 222. Which of these are below 29?
Model answer (advanced)Helium (4 g/mol) and neon (20 g/mol) — both lighter than air. Argon, krypton, xenon and radon are all heavier than air and sink.

7. Which noble gas balloon would sink fastest and most dramatically, earning it the nickname "lead balloon"?

HintThis is the noble gas at the very bottom of Group 0, with by far the greatest molar mass (222 g/mol).
Model answer (advanced)Radon. At 222 g/mol — nearly eight times heavier than air — a radon-filled balloon would drop like a stone, exactly like the idiom "goes down like a lead balloon."

B. Gas Tests — Mix and Match

Four gas tests you've carried out in the lab. Some questions give you the test and ask for the gas; others give you the gas and ask what you'd observe. Mix and match your way through all eight.

The four tests, summarised
  1. Hydrogen: hold a lit splint at the mouth of the test tube — a squeaky pop confirms hydrogen.
  2. Oxygen: hold a glowing splint in the test tube — it relights if the gas is oxygen.
  3. Carbon dioxide: bubble the gas through limewater — it turns cloudy (milky) if the gas is carbon dioxide.
  4. Chlorine: hold damp blue litmus paper in the gas — it turns red, then is bleached white.

1. Which gas gives a "squeaky pop" when a lit splint is held to it?

HintThis is the lightest of the four gases, and the one you may have made by reacting a metal with an acid.
Model answer (advanced)Hydrogen. The pop is the sound of the hydrogen igniting and burning almost instantly in air.

2. Which gas relights a glowing splint?

HintThis is the gas that supports combustion.
Model answer (advanced)Oxygen. The glowing splint bursts back into flame because it has enough oxygen to reignite.

3. Which gas turns limewater cloudy (milky) when bubbled through it?

HintThis is the gas you tested with calcium hydroxide solution.
Model answer (advanced)Carbon dioxide. The cloudiness is a fine white precipitate of calcium carbonate forming in the limewater.

4. Which gas turns damp blue litmus paper red, and then bleaches it white?

HintThis gas is a bleaching agent as well as being acidic.
Model answer (advanced)Chlorine. The red colour appears first (chlorine dissolving in the moisture forms an acidic solution), then the colour is bleached out entirely.

5. What would you observe if you held a lit splint at the mouth of a test tube of hydrogen gas?

HintLook back at test 1 above.
Model answer (advanced)A squeaky pop — the sound of the hydrogen igniting rapidly.

6. What would you observe if you held a glowing splint inside a test tube of oxygen gas?

HintLook back at test 2 above.
Model answer (advanced)The glowing splint relights, bursting back into flame.

7. What would you observe if you bubbled carbon dioxide gas through limewater?

HintLook back at test 3 above.
Model answer (advanced)The limewater turns cloudy (milky), as a white precipitate of calcium carbonate forms.

8. What would you observe if you held damp blue litmus paper in a test tube of chlorine gas?

HintLook back at test 4 above — there are two stages to the colour change.
Model answer (advanced)The litmus paper first turns red, then is bleached white entirely.

C. Explaining Ionic Formulae

These questions aren't self-marking — there's no single "correct" sentence. Write your own explanation in each box, using the ion charges to justify the formula, then reveal the model answer to compare.

1. Explain why the formula for magnesium chloride is MgCl₂, and not MgCl. Discuss in terms of the charges on the magnesium and chloride ions.

Model answerMagnesium forms the ion Mg²⁺ (it loses 2 electrons), and chlorine forms the ion Cl⁻ (it gains 1 electron). For the overall compound to be electrically neutral, the positive and negative charges must cancel out — so two Cl⁻ ions (total charge 2⁻) are needed to balance one Mg²⁺ ion (charge 2+), giving the formula MgCl₂.

2. Explain why the formula for aluminium oxide is Al₂O₃. Discuss in terms of ion charges.

Model answerAluminium forms Al³⁺ and oxygen forms O²⁻. The charges (3+ and 2⁻) don't cancel in a simple 1:1 ratio, so you need the smallest numbers of each ion that balance exactly: two Al³⁺ ions give 6+ total, and three O²⁻ ions give 6⁻ total — equal and opposite, giving Al₂O₃.

3. Explain why the formula for sodium sulfate is Na₂SO₄, and not NaSO₄.

Model answerSodium forms the ion Na⁺, and sulfate is a group ion with charge SO₄²⁻. One Na⁺ only supplies 1+, which isn't enough to cancel the 2⁻ on the sulfate ion, so two Na⁺ ions (total 2+) are needed, giving Na₂SO₄.

4. Explain why the formula for calcium carbonate is CaCO₃, with a simple 1:1 ratio, even though calcium is a metal that forms a 2+ ion.

Model answerCalcium forms Ca²⁺, and carbonate is a group ion with charge CO₃²⁻. Because these two charges (2+ and 2⁻) are already equal and opposite, just one of each ion is needed to cancel the charge completely — no larger numbers are required, giving the simple formula CaCO₃.

5. Iron can form two different chlorides, FeCl₂ and FeCl₃. Explain, in terms of ion charges, why iron can have two different formulae with chlorine, when sodium only ever forms NaCl.

Model answerIron is a transition metal and can lose different numbers of electrons, forming either Fe²⁺ (iron(II)) or Fe³⁺ (iron(III)). Each needs a different number of Cl⁻ ions to balance its charge — two for Fe²⁺, three for Fe³⁺ — giving FeCl₂ and FeCl₃ respectively. Sodium, by contrast, only ever forms the single ion Na⁺, so it only ever needs one Cl⁻ and only ever forms NaCl.

6. Explain why the formula for ammonium sulfate is written (NH₄)₂SO₄, with brackets around the NH₄ group.

Model answerThe ammonium ion, NH₄⁺, and the sulfate ion, SO₄²⁻, need a ratio of 2:1 to balance their charges (two NH₄⁺ give 2+ total, matching the 2⁻ on one SO₄²⁻). Brackets are used around NH₄ because more than one whole ammonium group is needed — without them, it would be ambiguous which atoms the leading “2” is meant to multiply.

D. Predicting Precipitates: Carbonates and Hydroxides

The same precipitate-naming skill you've used before, applied to two families of insoluble compounds: carbonates and hydroxides. In each word equation, a soluble metal salt reacts with a soluble source of carbonate or hydroxide ions, swapping partners to leave an insoluble precipitate behind.

Solubility rules you need here All carbonates are insoluble, except those of the Group I metals (like sodium) and ammonium. Almost all hydroxides are insoluble, except those of the Group I metals, ammonium — and calcium hydroxide, which is unusual: it's only slightly soluble, giving the cloudy solution known as limewater rather than a proper precipitate.
Worked example magnesium nitrate + sodium carbonate → magnesium carbonate (the precipitate) + sodium nitrate

1. magnesium chloride + sodium carbonate → ______ + sodium chloride

HintSwap the metal ions between the two starting salts, as before.
Model answer (advanced)Magnesium carbonate — an insoluble white solid.

2. calcium nitrate + sodium carbonate → ______ + sodium nitrate

HintSwap the metal ions between the two starting salts.
Model answer (advanced)Calcium carbonate — the same insoluble compound found naturally as limestone and chalk.

3. zinc nitrate + sodium carbonate → ______ + sodium nitrate

HintSwap the metal ions between the two starting salts.
Model answer (advanced)Zinc carbonate — an insoluble white solid.

4. lead nitrate + sodium carbonate → ______ + sodium nitrate

HintLead's only common soluble salt is lead nitrate — that's why it's the one used here, rather than lead chloride or lead sulfate (both of which are themselves insoluble).
Model answer (advanced)Lead carbonate — an insoluble white solid.

5. magnesium sulfate + sodium hydroxide → ______ + sodium sulfate

HintSwap the metal ions between the two starting compounds, exactly as with the carbonates above.
Model answer (advanced)Magnesium hydroxide — a white precipitate, the same compound (in suspension) as "milk of magnesia".

6. zinc sulfate + sodium hydroxide → ______ + sodium sulfate

HintSwap the metal ions between the two starting compounds.
Model answer (advanced)Zinc hydroxide — a white, gelatinous precipitate.

7. lead nitrate + sodium hydroxide → ______ + sodium nitrate

HintSwap the metal ions between the two starting compounds.
Model answer (advanced)Lead hydroxide — a white precipitate.

8. calcium nitrate + sodium hydroxide → ______ + sodium nitrate. Following the same pattern as the other hydroxides above, what would you actually observe here?

HintLook back at the solubility rules box at the top of this section — one hydroxide is named as the exception.
Model answer (advanced)Calcium hydroxide is the one hydroxide that doesn't follow the "insoluble" pattern the others do — it's only slightly soluble, so this reaction just produces the faintly cloudy solution known as limewater, not a solid precipitate you could filter out.
