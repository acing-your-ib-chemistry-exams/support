Activity 1: Molar Mass from Formulae — Practice

Working out the molar mass of a compound from its formula, one skill at a time: single elements, simple compounds, formulae with repeated atoms, and formulae with brackets. This is self-marking — work through it in any order, and use "Check" to see how you did, or "Hint" if you get stuck.

The method, every time

  1. Write out, or picture, every atom in the formula — including any hidden by a subscript or a bracket.
  2. Look up the relative atomic massThe mass of one atom of an element, compared with 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Found on the periodic table, usually the larger of the two numbers given for each element. (Ar) of each element involved.
  3. Multiply each Ar by how many times that atom appears in the formula.
  4. Add all the totals together. The answer is the molar massThe mass of one mole of a substance, measured in grams per mole (g/mol), found by adding together the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in its formula. "Molecular mass" is an older name for the same idea., in grams per mole (g/mol).

Glossary

Relative atomic mass (Ar)
The mass of one atom of an element, compared with 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Read straight off the periodic table.
Molar mass (molecular mass)
The mass of one mole of a substance, measured in grams per mole (g/mol), found by adding together the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in its formula. "Molecular mass" is an older name for the same idea.
Formula
The symbols and numbers showing the atoms present in one compound, for example CaCO₃.
Subscript
A small number written after an element's symbol, showing how many atoms of that element are present, for example the 2 in H₂O.
Bracket multiplier
A subscript written after a closing bracket, which multiplies every atom inside that bracket, for example the 2 in Ca(OH)₂ — there are two oxygens and two hydrogens, not one of each.
Diatomic molecule
A molecule made of two atoms of the same element joined together, such as O₂, Cl₂, N₂ or H₂.
Hydrate
A compound with a fixed number of water molecules built into its solid structure, shown with a raised dot in its formula, for example CuSO₄·5H₂O.

1. What is the Ar of carbon?

HintLook it up in the table above.
Model answer (advanced)Carbon's Ar of 12 is the reference point the whole relative atomic mass scale is built on — one atom of carbon-12 is defined as having a mass of exactly 12 units.

2. What is the Ar of chlorine?

HintThis is the one value on the table that isn't a whole number.
Model answer (advanced)Chlorine's Ar is not a whole number because naturally occurring chlorine is a mixture of two isotopes (chlorine-35 and chlorine-37); the Ar is the average, weighted by how common each isotope actually is.

3. What is the Ar of iron?

HintLook it up in the table above.
Model answer (advanced)Iron's Ar, 56, applies whichever ion it forms — iron(II) or iron(III) — since the charge comes from lost electrons, which have almost no mass of their own.

4. On a periodic table, an element's box usually shows two numbers. Which one is the relative atomic mass?

HintCompare chlorine's two numbers: 17 and 35.5. Which one did you just use?
Model answer (advanced)The smaller, always whole number is the atomic number (protons); the larger number, which can include a decimal, is the relative atomic mass — easy to confuse them until you notice one is always a whole number and the other usually isn't.

5. What is the Ar of potassium?

HintLook it up in the table above.
Model answer (advanced)Potassium and calcium sit next to each other but their Ar values are in the "wrong" order compared with atomic number, one of a handful of places on the real periodic table where mass and atomic number don't quite line up in step.

B. Elements and diatomic molecules simplest case

The molar mass of a single element, on its own, is just its Ar. Some elements, though, don't exist as single atoms — oxygen, chlorine, nitrogen and hydrogen are all found as diatomic moleculesA molecule made of two atoms of the same element joined together, such as O₂, Cl₂, N₂ or H₂., two atoms joined together, so their formula needs a subscript 2 even though only one element is involved.

Worked example Molar mass of O₂: there are 2 oxygen atoms, each with Ar = 16.
  1. 2 × 16 = 32
Molar mass of O₂ = 32 g/mol.

6. What is the molar mass of iron, Fe, as a lump of the metal (just one atom at a time)?

HintIron isn't diatomic — this is just its Ar.
Model answer (advanced)Metals like iron don't form small molecules at all — a piece of iron is a giant lattice of atoms, so "molar mass" here just means the mass of one mole of separate Fe atoms.

7. What is the molar mass of chlorine gas, Cl₂?

HintTwo chlorine atoms: 2 × 35.5.
Model answer (advanced)Chlorine's molecular molar mass, 71 g/mol, is roughly two and a half times heavier than air — part of why chlorine gas sinks and can be collected by downward delivery.

8. What is the molar mass of nitrogen gas, N₂ (the main gas in the air around you)?

HintTwo nitrogen atoms: 2 × 14.
Model answer (advanced)N₂'s two nitrogen atoms are held together by a very strong triple bond — part of why nitrogen gas is so unreactive, and why turning it into useful compounds (as in the Haber process) takes real effort.

9. What is the molar mass of hydrogen gas, H₂?

HintTwo hydrogen atoms: 2 × 1.
Model answer (advanced)At just 2 g/mol, hydrogen gas is the lightest molecule that exists — this low molar mass is exactly why it produces that characteristic "squeaky pop" and rises so readily.

10. What is the molar mass of sodium metal, Na?

HintSodium is a metal, not diatomic — this is just its Ar.
Model answer (advanced)Like iron, sodium metal is a lattice of single atoms, so its molar mass is simply its Ar — no doubling needed.

C. Simple compounds two elements, one of each

Now for compounds — start with the simplest kind, where the formula has just one atom of each element.

Worked example Molar mass of NaCl: 1 sodium (Ar = 23) + 1 chlorine (Ar = 35.5).
  1. 23 + 35.5 = 58.5
Molar mass of NaCl = 58.5 g/mol.

11. What is the molar mass of magnesium oxide, MgO?

Hint24 + 16.
Model answer (advanced)Magnesium oxide's molar mass, 40 g/mol, happens to equal calcium's Ar exactly — a coincidence worth noticing so you don't mix the two up when they turn up in the same question.

12. What is the molar mass of calcium oxide, CaO?

Hint40 + 16.
Model answer (advanced)Calcium oxide's molar mass, 56 g/mol, also happens to match iron's Ar — another pair worth keeping separate in your head, since they'll come up in different contexts.

13. What is the molar mass of potassium bromide, KBr?

Hint39 + 80.
Model answer (advanced)Bromine's Ar, 80, is one worth double-checking on the table each time — it's easy to confuse with barium's 137 or silver's 108 when working quickly.

D. Repeated atoms watch the subscripts

When a subscript sits directly after an element's symbol (with no bracket), it multiplies just that one element, not the whole formula.

Worked example Molar mass of CO₂: 1 carbon (Ar = 12) + 2 oxygens (Ar = 16 each).
  1. 1 × 12 = 12
  2. 2 × 16 = 32
  3. 12 + 32 = 44
Molar mass of CO₂ = 44 g/mol.

14. What is the molar mass of sulfur trioxide, SO₃?

Hint32 + (3 × 16).
Model answer (advanced)SO₃ is the gas at the heart of the Contact process, which turns it into sulfuric acid — its molar mass matters directly when working out how much acid a factory can produce from a given mass of sulfur.

15. What is the molar mass of dinitrogen pentoxide, N₂O₅?

Hint(2 × 14) + (5 × 16).
Model answer (advanced)Both subscripts have to be doubled and multiplied separately here — a good check that you're multiplying each element by its own subscript, not just adding every number you can see.

16. What is the molar mass of methane, CH₄?

Hint12 + (4 × 1).
Model answer (advanced)Methane's low molar mass, 16 g/mol, is one reason it's a gas at room temperature — small, light molecules generally have lower boiling points than large, heavy ones.

17. What is the molar mass of ammonia, NH₃?

Hint14 + (3 × 1).
Model answer (advanced)Ammonia, NH₃, is the actual product of the Haber process — N₂ and H₂ (both from Section B) combine to make it, so its molar mass connects two ideas you've already practised on this page.

E. Brackets and group ions this week's focus

A subscript written after a closing bracket multiplies everything inside the bracket — not just the atom next to it.

Worked example Molar mass of Ca(OH)₂: 1 calcium (Ar = 40), and the bracket (OH) — 1 oxygen (16) + 1 hydrogen (1) — appears twice.
  1. Calcium: 1 × 40 = 40
  2. Inside the bracket: 16 + 1 = 17
  3. Multiply the bracket by 2: 2 × 17 = 34
  4. Add: 40 + 34 = 74
Molar mass of Ca(OH)₂ = 74 g/mol.

18. What is the molar mass of magnesium nitrate, Mg(NO₃)₂?

HintInside the bracket: 14 + (3 × 16) = 62. Then double it before adding magnesium.
Model answer (advanced)24 + 2×(14 + 48) = 24 + 124 = 148 g/mol. Note that "2" outside the bracket doubles the whole NO₃ group — three oxygens and one nitrogen, twice over.

19. What is the molar mass of aluminium sulfate, Al₂(SO₄)₃?

HintTwo subscripts to handle here: aluminium is ×2, and the whole (SO₄) bracket is ×3.
Model answer (advanced)(2 × 27) + 3×(32 + 64) = 54 + 288 = 342 g/mol. This is the compound with two multipliers working at once — the most challenging pattern on this page, so it's worth slowing right down for.

20. What is the molar mass of ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄?

HintThe bracket (NH₄) is doubled; SO₄ on its own is not.
Model answer (advanced)2×(14 + 4) + (32 + 64) = 36 + 96 = 132 g/mol. Ammonium sulfate is a common nitrogen fertiliser — its molar mass is exactly what a farmer, or a factory, needs to work out how much nitrogen a given mass of it actually delivers.

21. What is the molar mass of calcium phosphate, Ca₃(PO₄)₂?

HintCalcium is ×3; the (PO₄) bracket is ×2. Ar of phosphorus is 31.
Model answer (advanced)3×40 + 2×(31 + 64) = 120 + 190 = 310 g/mol. Calcium phosphate is the main mineral in bones and teeth, and also the starting point for making phosphate fertilisers.

22. What is the molar mass of barium nitrate, Ba(NO₃)₂?

Hint137 + 2×(14 + 48).
Model answer (advanced)137 + 2×62 = 137 + 124 = 261 g/mol. Barium compounds burn with a distinctive green flame — a useful fact if you ever meet this compound again in a flame-test context.

F. Mixed practice consolidation

These are all compounds you've met before, in the lab or in earlier practice — a chance to put every pattern from this page to use.

23. What is the molar mass of copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄?

Hint64 + 32 + (4 × 16).
Model answer (advanced)You'll meet CuSO₄'s molar mass, 160 g/mol, again in the bonus round, once water is added to its structure.

24. What is the molar mass of zinc sulfate, ZnSO₄?

Hint65 + 32 + (4 × 16).
Model answer (advanced)ZnSO₄ and CuSO₄ differ by just one g/mol (161 versus 160) — proof that zinc and copper's Ar values, 65 and 64, are close enough to catch you out if you're not careful reading the table.

25. What is the molar mass of iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃ (the compound in rust)?

Hint(2 × 56) + (3 × 16).
Model answer (advanced)Fe₂O₃'s molar mass also comes to 160 g/mol — the same number as copper(II) sulfate in question 23, purely by coincidence, and a reminder that the final answer alone doesn't tell you which compound you started with.

26. What is the molar mass of silver nitrate, AgNO₃?

Hint108 + 14 + (3 × 16). Note there's no bracket needed, since there's only one NO₃ group.
Model answer (advanced)Because there's only one nitrate group, no bracket is needed at all — a useful reminder that brackets only appear once a group ion is repeated more than once.

27. What is the molar mass of sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃?

Hint(2 × 23) + 12 + (3 × 16).
Model answer (advanced)You may recognise this one — forgetting to double sodium's contribution before adding carbon and the three oxygens is the single most common slip in this kind of calculation.

G. Spot the mistake diagnostic

Each question shows a calculation someone actually got wrong. Work out what went wrong before you answer.

28. A student calculated the molar mass of Mg(NO₃)₂ like this: "Mg = 24, N = 14, O = 3 × 16 = 48, total = 24 + 14 + 48 = 86." What did they get wrong?

HintCompare this to your working for question 18 — what step is missing here?
Model answer (advanced)The correct working doubles the entire (NO₃) group, not just one part of it: 24 + 2×(14 + 48) = 148 g/mol, the same compound and answer as question 18 — 62 g/mol short of the correct total.

29. A student worked out the molar mass of CO₂ as "12 + 16 = 28," treating the subscript as if it wasn't there. What is the correct molar mass?

HintThere are two oxygens in CO₂, not one — look back at the worked example in Section D.
Model answer (advanced)28 g/mol happens to be the correct molar mass of a completely different molecule, N₂ (question 8) — a good illustration of why it's worth double-checking your working, not just whether the final number "looks about right."

30. A student calculated (NH₄)₂SO₄ as "N = 14, H = 4, S = 32, O = 4 × 16 = 64, total = 114." What did they forget?

HintCompare this to your working for question 20.
Model answer (advanced)The correct total is 132 g/mol (question 20) — 18 g/mol more than the mistaken working, exactly the mass of one extra NH₄ group (14 + 4) that was missed.

31. A student worked out the molar mass of Ca(OH)₂ as "40 + 16 + 1 = 57," forgetting the bracket multiplier entirely. What is the correct molar mass?

HintLook back at the worked example at the top of Section E.
Model answer (advanced)The correct answer, 74 g/mol, is 17 g/mol more than the mistaken one — precisely the mass of the second, forgotten (OH) group.

H. Terminology check consolidation

These use the exact wording from the glossary at the top of this page.

32. Which is the correct definition of molar mass?

HintThis is the definition at the very top of the glossary.
Model answer (advanced)"Molecular mass" is an older name for exactly the same idea, so don't be thrown if you see that phrase used instead in an older textbook or worksheet.

33. Which is the correct definition of relative atomic mass (Ar)?

HintThis is the value you read straight off the periodic table.
Model answer (advanced)Ar is always a comparison, not an absolute mass — which is why it has no units at all, unlike molar mass, which is measured in g/mol.

34. What unit is molar mass always measured in?

HintLook at the end of the molar mass definition in the glossary.
Model answer (advanced)Every single molar mass calculation on this page has this same unit — it never changes, only the number in front of it does.

35. What is the name for a subscript written after a closing bracket, which multiplies everything inside it?

HintIt's the term used throughout Section E.
Model answer (advanced)This is exactly the idea Section E's worked example and question 31's "spot the mistake" both hinge on — a bracket multiplier applies to every atom inside, not just the one nearest it.

36. What word describes a molecule made of two atoms of the same element joined together, such as O₂ or Cl₂?

Hint"Di-" means two.
Model answer (advanced)Only a handful of elements exist this way at room temperature — the ones you practised in Section B (O₂, Cl₂, N₂, H₂) are the ones you'll meet most often.

Bonus round stretch

A harder look at the same idea: a hydrate, a calculation run in reverse, and a direct comparison.

Worked example — a hydrate Copper(II) sulfate crystals are actually CuSO₄·5H₂O — the raised dot means 5 whole water molecules are built into the solid structure, and they count too.
  1. CuSO₄ (from question 23) = 160
  2. 5 × H₂O (each H₂O = 18) = 90
  3. 160 + 90 = 250
Molar mass of CuSO₄·5H₂O = 250 g/mol.

37. Using the worked example above, what is the molar mass of CuSO₄·5H₂O?

HintIt's worked out for you just above — add the two totals together.
Model answer (advanced)Almost a third of a hydrate's total mass can be water of crystallisation — which is why heating blue copper(II) sulfate crystals to drive the water off causes such a dramatic mass loss and colour change, from blue to white.

38. A compound has the formula MgX₂ and a molar mass of 95 g/mol. Using Ar(Mg) = 24, what is the Ar of X?

HintSubtract magnesium's Ar from the total, then remember there are two X atoms, not one.
Model answer (advanced)(95 − 24) ÷ 2 = 35.5 — this is exactly the calculation you'd run backwards to identify an unknown element from a measured molar mass, which is how X-ray or mass-spectrometry data is sometimes used in real analytical chemistry.

39. Using your answer to question 38, which element is X, and so what compound is MgX₂ actually?

HintWhich element on the Ar table in Section A has this exact value?
Model answer (advanced)MgX₂ turns out to be MgCl₂, magnesium chloride — an ordinary formula you've likely built with the criss-cross method before, arrived at here from the opposite direction, mass first.

40. Without recalculating from scratch, which has the greater molar mass: sodium hydroxide, NaOH, or magnesium sulfate, MgSO₄?

HintNaOH's molar mass is 40 g/mol. Work out MgSO₄'s and compare.
Model answer (advanced)MgSO₄ = 24 + 32 + 64 = 120 g/mol, three times NaOH's 40 g/mol — a useful check that a heavier-looking formula, with more atoms and higher subscripts, generally does mean a heavier mole, though it's always worth confirming by calculation rather than guessing from the formula's length alone.
