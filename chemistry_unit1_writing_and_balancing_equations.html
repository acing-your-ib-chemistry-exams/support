Activity 5: Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

You've already predicted the missing precipitate in a word equation. Now the whole reaction gets written properly: first every compound in words becomes a correct formula, then those formulae are arranged into a full equation — and finally, numbers are added in front so that the equation obeys the golden rule of chemistry: atoms are never created or destroyed. Self-marking, with hints, and extra practice folded in automatically if a question doesn't go your way.

Glossary

Reactant
A substance present at the start of a reaction, written on the left of the arrow.
Product
A substance formed by a reaction, written on the right of the arrow.
Precipitate
An insoluble solid that forms and separates out from a solution during a reaction.
Balanced equation
An equation in which the number of atoms of each element is exactly the same on both sides of the arrow.
Coefficient
A number placed in front of a whole formula to show how many units of it react or form, e.g. the "2" in 2H₂O. It multiplies everything in that formula, but never changes the formula itself.
Law of conservation of mass
In a chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed — so the total mass of the reactants always equals the total mass of the products. This is the whole reason an equation must be balanced.

A. Review: naming the precipitate already covered

A quick reminder before we go further: two soluble salts, mixed together, can make an insoluble solid — a precipitate — plus a second soluble salt left in solution. Name just the precipitate, exactly as before.

Worked example — the one you made magnesium sulfate + sodium carbonate → magnesium carbonate (the precipitate) + sodium sulfate

1. calcium chloride + sodium carbonate → ______ + sodium chloride

HintSwap the metal ions between the two starting salts.
Model answer (advanced)Calcium carbonate — the same insoluble compound found naturally as limestone and chalk.

2. copper sulfate + sodium hydroxide → ______ + sodium sulfate

HintThis one forms a pale blue gelatinous precipitate.
Model answer (advanced)Copper(II) hydroxide — a classic gelatinous, pale blue precipitate.

3. zinc nitrate + sodium hydroxide → ______ + sodium nitrate

HintFollow the same swapping pattern as the copper example.
Model answer (advanced)Zinc hydroxide — another gelatinous precipitate, this time from zinc ions.

4. barium chloride + sodium sulfate → ______ + sodium chloride

HintThis one is very well known for being insoluble.
Model answer (advanced)Barium sulfate — so insoluble that it's used in medicine for "barium meal" X-ray imaging.

B. Words into formulae new idea

Naming the precipitate in words is only half the job. To write a proper chemical equation, every compound needs to become a correct formula — reactants and products alike — using the ionic charges you already know.

Worked example — every compound, translated calcium chloride + sodium carbonate → calcium carbonate + sodium chloride
  1. Calcium chloride: Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ → CaCl₂
  2. Sodium carbonate: Na⁺ and CO₃²⁻ → Na₂CO₃
  3. Calcium carbonate: Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃
  4. Sodium chloride: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ → NaCl
Formula equation (not yet balanced) = CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + NaCl.

Now do the same for the other reactions from Section A. Copper is 2+, hydroxide is 1−; zinc is 2+, nitrate is 1−; barium is 2+, sulfate is 2−.

5. copper sulfate + sodium hydroxide → copper hydroxide + sodium sulfate. What is the correct formula for copper hydroxide?

HintCopper is 2+, hydroxide is 1− — you'll need two hydroxides, bracketed.
Model answer (advanced)Cu(OH)₂ — the brackets show that the whole OH group is doubled, not just one atom inside it.

6. In the same reaction, what is the correct formula for sodium sulfate?

HintSodium is 1+, sulfate is 2− — you'll need two sodiums.
Model answer (advanced)Na₂SO₄.

7. zinc nitrate + sodium hydroxide → zinc hydroxide + sodium nitrate. What is the correct formula for zinc nitrate?

HintZinc is 2+, nitrate is 1− — you'll need two nitrates, bracketed.
Model answer (advanced)Zn(NO₃)₂ — the brackets keep the whole NO₃ group together when it's doubled.

8. In the same reaction, what is the correct formula for zinc hydroxide?

HintSame pattern as copper hydroxide — zinc is also 2+.
Model answer (advanced)Zn(OH)₂.

9. barium chloride + sodium sulfate → barium sulfate + sodium chloride. What is the correct formula for barium sulfate?

HintBarium is 2+, sulfate is 2− — the charges cancel exactly.
Model answer (advanced)BaSO₄ — equal, opposite charges mean no subscript is needed at all.

10. In the same reaction, what is the correct formula for barium chloride?

HintBarium is 2+, chloride is 1− — you'll need two chlorides.
Model answer (advanced)BaCl₂.

Now translate a whole new reaction — one you'll be balancing shortly. Magnesium carbonate reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid, just like the "pill" reaction you carried out before, producing magnesium chloride, water and carbon dioxide.

11. What is the correct formula for magnesium carbonate?

HintMagnesium is 2+, carbonate is 2− — the charges cancel exactly.
Model answer (advanced)MgCO₃.

12. What is the correct formula for hydrochloric acid?

HintOne hydrogen, one chloride.
Model answer (advanced)HCl.

13. What is the correct formula for magnesium chloride?

HintMagnesium is 2+, chloride is 1− — you'll need two chlorides.
Model answer (advanced)MgCl₂.

14. What is the correct formula for water?

HintTwo hydrogens, one oxygen.
Model answer (advanced)H₂O.

15. What is the correct formula for carbon dioxide?

HintOne carbon, two oxygens — this is the same gas you tested with limewater.
Model answer (advanced)CO₂. So the unbalanced formula equation is MgCO₃ + HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ — you'll balance this one shortly.

C. Why equations must balance the idea

Every formula equation you just wrote has a problem: as written, the number of atoms doesn't always match on both sides. That matters because of the law of conservation of massIn a chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed, so the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products. — atoms aren't created or destroyed in a reaction, only rearranged. So a correctly written equation must have exactly the same number of each type of atom on both sides. That's what "balancing" means.

The only tool you're allowed to use is a coefficient — a number placed in front of a whole formula. You can never change a subscript to balance an equation, because that would turn the substance into something else entirely: H₂O₂ (hydrogen peroxide) is not "extra-balanced water" — it's a completely different compound.

16. In the equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, what does the "2" in front of H₂O actually mean?

HintA coefficient sits outside and in front of the whole formula — it multiplies the formula, it doesn't sit inside it.
Model answer (advanced)A coefficient scales up the whole formula as a unit; a subscript changes what the formula actually is. Mixing these two up is the single most common balancing mistake.

17. The law of conservation of mass says that in a chemical reaction, ______ are neither created nor destroyed. (Fill the blank with one word.)

HintThink about what a coefficient counts.
Model answer (advanced)Atoms. Since no atoms appear or disappear during a reaction, the total mass can't change either — which is why an unbalanced equation is actually a claim that mass was created or destroyed, which is never true.

D. Balancing simple equations start here

Method: count the atoms of each element on both sides. If one doesn't match, place a coefficient in front of a formula — never change a subscript — then recount everything, since fixing one element can unbalance another. Keep going until every element matches. A coefficient of 1 is never written.

Worked example Magnesium reacts with oxygen to form magnesium oxide: Mg + O₂ → MgO.
  1. Count: left has 1 Mg, 2 O. Right has 1 Mg, 1 O. Oxygen doesn't match.
  2. Place a 2 in front of MgO: Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Recount: left 1 Mg, 2 O; right 2 Mg, 2 O. Now magnesium doesn't match!
  3. Place a 2 in front of Mg: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Recount: left 2 Mg, 2 O; right 2 Mg, 2 O. Balanced.
Balanced equation = 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.

18. Balance this equation from Section B: MgCO₃ + ___HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. What coefficient goes in front of HCl?

HintCount chlorine on the right: MgCl₂ has 2. There's only 1 Cl on the left as written — how many HCl do you need?
Model answer (advanced)2. MgCO₃ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Checking hydrogen too: 2HCl gives 2 H on the left, and H₂O gives 2 H on the right — the same coefficient fixes both elements at once here.

19. Zinc reacts with hydrochloric acid: Zn + ___HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. What coefficient goes in front of HCl?

HintZnCl₂ has 2 chlorines on the right. Each HCl only supplies 1.
Model answer (advanced)2. Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ — and checking hydrogen, 2HCl gives 2 H on the left, matching the 2 in H₂ on the right, so nothing else needs changing.

20. Magnesium burns in oxygen: Mg + O₂ → ___MgO. What coefficient goes in front of MgO, to balance the oxygen?

HintO₂ supplies 2 oxygen atoms. MgO only has 1 oxygen as written.
Model answer (advanced)2 — Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. But look what that does to magnesium — on to the next question.

21. Now that MgO has a coefficient of 2, magnesium no longer balances: ___Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. What coefficient goes in front of Mg?

HintThe right side now has 2 magnesiums (from 2MgO). The left side needs to match.
Model answer (advanced)2 — 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Exactly the worked example above: fixing oxygen first unbalanced magnesium, so you had to come back and fix that too.

22. Sodium burns in chlorine: Na + Cl₂ → ___NaCl. What coefficient goes in front of NaCl, to balance the chlorine?

HintCl₂ supplies 2 chlorine atoms. NaCl only has 1 chlorine as written.
Model answer (advanced)2 — Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. Check what happens to sodium next.

23. Now that NaCl has a coefficient of 2, sodium no longer balances: ___Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. What coefficient goes in front of Na?

HintThe right side now has 2 sodiums (from 2NaCl).
Model answer (advanced)2 — 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. Same pattern as magnesium and oxygen: a metal reacting with a diatomic non-metal very often needs this "fix one, then fix the other" two-step approach.

E. Balancing — more examples more practice

24. Nitrogen and hydrogen form ammonia (the Haber process): N₂ + H₂ → ___NH₃. What coefficient goes in front of NH₃, to balance the nitrogen?

HintN₂ supplies 2 nitrogen atoms. NH₃ only has 1 nitrogen as written.
Model answer (advanced)2 — N₂ + H₂ → 2NH₃. Now count the hydrogens on each side before moving on.

25. With NH₃ now at 2NH₃, the right side has 6 hydrogens (2 × 3). N₂ + ___H₂ → 2NH₃. What coefficient goes in front of H₂?

HintEach H₂ supplies 2 hydrogens. How many H₂ molecules make 6 hydrogens?
Model answer (advanced)3 — N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. This is the real, famous Haber process equation, run at huge scale to make the ammonia used in fertiliser.

26. Methane burns in oxygen: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + ___H₂O. What coefficient goes in front of H₂O, to balance the hydrogen?

HintCH₄ supplies 4 hydrogens. Each H₂O only supplies 2.
Model answer (advanced)2 — CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Now count the oxygens before moving on.
Since that one didn't land, try this before moving on — skip it if you'd rather push ahead.

26a. Carbon burns in a limited supply of oxygen to form carbon monoxide: C + O₂ → ___CO. What coefficient goes in front of CO, to balance the oxygen?

HintO₂ supplies 2 oxygens. CO only supplies 1 as written.
Model answer (advanced)2 — C + O₂ → 2CO. Carbon then needs a coefficient of 2 as well, giving 2C + O₂ → 2CO — the same "fix one, then the other" pattern again.

27. With H₂O now at 2H₂O, count the oxygens: CO₂ supplies 2, and 2H₂O supplies another 2, making 4 in total on the right. CH₄ + ___O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. What coefficient goes in front of O₂?

HintEach O₂ supplies 2 oxygens. How many O₂ molecules make 4 oxygens?
Model answer (advanced)2 — CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. This is the equation for burning natural gas (methane), whether in a Bunsen burner or a gas boiler.

28. Hydrogen reacts with chlorine: H₂ + Cl₂ → ___HCl. What coefficient goes in front of HCl?

HintH₂ and Cl₂ each supply 2 atoms. HCl only supplies 1 of each as written.
Model answer (advanced)2 — H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. Unusually, only one coefficient is needed here — both hydrogen and chlorine balance at exactly the same time.
Since that one didn't land, try this before moving on — skip it if you'd rather push ahead.

28a. Zinc reacts with sulfur: Zn + S → ___ZnS. What coefficient goes in front of ZnS?

HintCount the atoms on each side as written — are they already equal?
Model answer (advanced)1 (which is why nothing is actually written there). Zn + S → ZnS is already balanced — not every equation needs a coefficient bigger than 1.

F. Spot the mistake diagnostic

29. Trying to balance H₂ + O₂ → H₂O, a student changes the formula to H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂. What's wrong with this?

HintOnly coefficients — numbers in front of a formula — are allowed when balancing.
Model answer (advanced)H₂O₂ is a real, different substance (hydrogen peroxide) — editing a subscript to "balance" an equation actually just describes a different reaction with different chemicals. The correct fix is 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

30. A student balances the oxygen in Mg + O₂ → MgO by writing Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, and stops there. What have they forgotten to do?

HintCount magnesium on both sides of Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.
Model answer (advanced)The right side of Mg + O₂ → 2MgO now has 2 magnesiums, but the left still has only 1 — balancing is never really finished until every single element has been recounted and matches.

G. Terminology consolidation

31. Which is the correct definition of a coefficient?

HintOption B is actually describing a subscript, not a coefficient — they're easy to mix up.
Model answer (advanced)A coefficient sits in front of and outside the formula, multiplying the whole thing — unlike a subscript, which is fixed as part of the formula itself.

32. Which is the correct definition of a balanced equation?

HintLook back at question 28a — that equation was balanced despite having no coefficients above 1.
Model answer (advanced)The number of substances on each side, and the size of the coefficients, are irrelevant on their own — what matters is that every individual element's atom count matches.

33. The small number written after an element's symbol inside a formula, like the 2 in CaCl₂, is called a ______.

HintThis is the term this whole page has been warning you never to change.
Model answer (advanced)Subscript — fixed by the formula itself, and never touched when balancing an equation.

34. What word describes an insoluble solid that forms and separates out from a solution during a reaction?

HintThis is the word Section A was all about.
Model answer (advanced)Precipitate — like the magnesium carbonate, copper hydroxide, zinc hydroxide and barium sulfate from Section A.

Bonus round going further

These two need three coefficients each, not just one or two — a taste of the harder balancing you'll meet in future activities.

35. Aluminium reacts with oxygen: Al + O₂ → ___Al₂O₃. What coefficient goes in front of Al₂O₃, so that the oxygen count (a multiple of 2, from O₂) matches a multiple of 3 (from Al₂O₃)?

HintYou need the smallest number that is both a multiple of 2 and a multiple of 3 — that's 6 oxygens. How many Al₂O₃ (each with 3 oxygens) gives 6?
Model answer (advanced)2 — giving 2Al₂O₃, which supplies 6 oxygens (a multiple of both 2 and 3, so O₂'s coefficient can now be a whole number too).

36. Al + ___O₂ → 2Al₂O₃. Now that the right side has 6 oxygens, what coefficient goes in front of O₂?

HintEach O₂ supplies 2 oxygens. How many O₂ molecules make 6?
Model answer (advanced)3 — Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃. Now count the aluminium before the final step.

37. ___Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃. The right side now has 4 aluminiums (2 × 2). What coefficient goes in front of Al?

HintMatch the 4 aluminiums now shown on the right.
Model answer (advanced)4 — 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃. Three different coefficients, all needed together — this is exactly the kind of equation later activities will give you plenty more practice with.

38. Iron reacts with oxygen to form iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃. Which of these is the correctly balanced equation?

HintCount iron and oxygen separately in each option — only one has both matching exactly.
Model answer (advanced)4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ — the same three-coefficient pattern as aluminium and oxygen, since iron(III) oxide and aluminium oxide have the same ratio of metal to oxygen.
