The Periodic Table in 1830

A companion to The Periodic Table in 1800, picked up thirty years on: this is the table as a chemist entering Liebig's Gießen laboratory in the 1830s would have known it, extended down to lead (Pb) and as far as uranium (U). Elements 93 and above are 20th-century, laboratory-made additions and are left off entirely: no version of chemistry in this period, however lucky, could have found them.

Forty-one more years — the whole span of Part 2 — and a turning point in how elements were found at all. 53 of these 92 elements were known, or partly known, by 1830 (50 confirmed outright, 3 still only a detected “earth”: titanium, uranium and thorium) — the rest, including every alkali metal below sodium and potassium, most of the platinum group, and every lanthanide, were still waiting to be found.
62 of these 92 elements were known, or partly known, by 1871 (59 confirmed outright, 3 still only a detected “earth” or spectral trace) — nine more than in 1830. The 50 elements known outright by 1830

Twenty-five of these go back to 1800 or earlier — see The Periodic Table in 1800 for that half of the story in full: the nine ancient metals and carbon and sulfur, arsenic and antimony from the medieval and early-modern periods, and a run of 18th-century isolations from cobalt (1735) through bismuth (1753) to chromium (1797).

The other twenty-five arrived in a single, frantic three-decade stretch after 1800, once Volta's electric pile (announced March 1800) and increasingly rigorous mineral analysis gave chemists two new tools at once. Vanadium and niobium turned up in 1801, tantalum in 1802, then a rush of five metals — cerium, palladium, rhodium, osmium, iridium — in 1803–04 alone, several of them teased out of the very same crate of crude South American platinum ore that William Hyde Wollaston and Smithson Tennant were dissecting in London. Humphry Davy then spent 1807–08 taking an electric pile to every stubborn “earth” in the cabinet and walked away with sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium, one after another. Iodine followed in 1811, lithium, cadmium and selenium in 1817, silicon in 1824, aluminium (impure) in 1825, and bromine in 1826.

Three of these twenty-five have a gold ring, because they were German finds, made by chemists working in exactly the university and state-laboratory culture this course keeps returning to. Cerium (1803) was found independently and almost simultaneously by Berzelius and Hisinger in Sweden and by Martin Heinrich Klaproth in Berlin. Cadmium (1817) was isolated by Friedrich Stromeyer, a chemistry professor at Göttingen, while investigating why a batch of zinc carbonate looked slightly off-colour. And beryllium metal (1828) was produced independently, within weeks of each other, by Friedrich Wöhler in Germany and Antoine Bussy in France. None of this is a coincidence: German universities were building exactly the kind of well-equipped teaching and research laboratories in this period that Liebig would go on to make famous at Gießen — see Part 2 of this reading series — and mineral analysis was one of the things those laboratories were best at.

The 3 half-known “earths” still not isolated (marked with *)

  • Titanium (1791). William Gregor, and independently Klaproth, found its oxide; a truly pure sample of the metal wasn't produced until 1910, over a century of stubborn resistance later.
  • Uranium (1789). Klaproth found a dense “earth” in pitchblende ore and named it after the planet Uranus. The metal itself would not be isolated until 1841 — watch this tile when you click through to 1871.
  • Thorium (1828). Berzelius found its “earth” in a Norwegian mineral just two years before this table's cut-off, and named it after the Norse god of thunder. The metal would stay unisolated for decades yet, so this tile doesn't change even by 1871.

What's still missing by 1830

Every noble gas (helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon) is absent, because none of them react chemically — there was no reaction available in this period that could have revealed one. Most of the second and third rows of transition metals (ruthenium, rhenium, technetium and their neighbours) and every lanthanide except cerium are absent too, still bound up in compounds and ores too closely alike for any 1830s method to tease apart. And the heavier alkali metals — rubidium and caesium — sit below sodium and potassium looking exactly like they ought to exist, but nothing in the chemist's toolkit of 1830 could yet prove it.

Click through to 1871 — when the toolkit itself changed

Press the button above the table and nine elements light up red, but they don't arrive the way the 1801–1830 rush did. The first four keep faith with the old method — mineral analysis, patient and slow. Carl Gustaf Mosander in Stockholm spent the 1839–43 window quietly proving that the tidy single “earths” found decades earlier were themselves mixtures: lanthanum split off from cerium in 1839, then terbium and erbium both split off from yttrium's yttria in 1843, all three named for hiding in plain sight or for the village of Ytterby that had already lent its name to yttrium itself. In 1841 uranium finally gets a metal to go with its 1789 “earth,” when Eugène-Melchior Péligot in Paris reduces uranium tetrachloride with potassium — watch that tile flip from partial to fully known. And in 1844 Karl Ernst Claus, working in Kazan in the Russian Empire, finds ruthenium in platinum-ore residue that London chemists had already picked over twice and thrown away as waste — the last of the six platinum-group metals, forty years after the first five.

Then, in 1859–60, the method itself changes. Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff, working together at Heidelberg, build a spectroscope — and Bunsen's own gas burner, designed for a clean, colourless flame, turns out to be exactly the tool that makes it work. Heat any substance hot enough and its atoms give off light only at certain fixed, sharp colours, an optical fingerprint unique to each element; split that light through a prism finely enough and a new fingerprint means a new element, even if there's only a trace of it in the sample. Caesium (1860) and rubidium (1861) are the first two elements ever found this way — both at Heidelberg, both named for the colour of their spectral lines (sky-blue and deep-red), both carrying the gold ring. Thallium (1861, William Crookes in London, with Claude-Auguste Lamy in France close behind) and indium (1863, Ferdinand Reich and Hieronymus Theodor Richter at the Freiberg mining academy in Saxony) followed within three years, proof the method wasn't a one-off. Reich, notably, was colour-blind and had to have his assistant Richter read the spectral lines for him — which is how indium ended up with two names on it rather than one.

The dashed-red tile is the strangest of all. On 18 August 1868, the French astronomer Pierre Jules César Janssen pointed a spectroscope at the Sun's chromosphere during a total eclipse and recorded a yellow line matching no element known on Earth; Norman Lockyer in London confirmed it soon after and, with the chemist Edward Frankland, named the substance helium, after the Greek for sun. By 1871 nobody had touched it, weighed it, or reacted it with anything — it existed only as a line of light from ninety-three million miles away, exactly the “detected but not isolated” status this table marks in dashed red for titanium and thorium too, just arrived at by an entirely new road. Helium wasn't found on Earth for another twenty-seven years.
