1.1 How industrialised was Germany in 1800? The chemical trades of the Rhine and the Main, from the old Empire to the first railway

Before we can ask how the great chemical works of the Rhine–Main valley came to be, we have to look at what was actually there before them. The answer is surprising, and it is not the answer the phrase “industrial revolution” leads you to expect.

Reading 1.1 1775 – September 1825 Frankfurt · Höchst · Mainz · Wiesbaden · Bad Homburg · Oberursel

Stand on the Alte Brücke in Frankfurt in the year 1800 — and in 1800 you did not need to say which bridge, because it was the only one across the Main — and look around. You are in one of the richest trading cities in the German-speaking world. And there is not one chemical factory anywhere in sight — nor in Höchst, nor Mainz, nor Wiesbaden, nor anywhere else along this river.

That is where this course starts. The dye works at Höchst, whose chimneys you can see from the train today, was founded in 1863. The story we are going to follow does not begin with factories. It begins with mills, salt pans, apothecary shops, boats being dragged upstream by horses, and a Europe at war with itself.

This reading covers fifty years, from 1775 — when the Holy Roman Empire still existed and Frankfurt was its own small country — to September 1825, when a railway opened in the north of England and quietly made everything in this reading obsolete.

Part one · 1775

A country that was not yet a country

In 1775 there was no Germany. There was the Holy Roman Empire, a patchwork of something like 300 states, free cities, church lands and knightly estates, all nominally under an emperor in Vienna who could not tell most of them what to do.

Our six towns were spread across four different countries, and you could walk between them in a day:

Look at those numbers again. Wiesbaden had about 2,200 people in 1800. Today it has around 280,000. In 1804, of its 400 households, 284 kept livestock and a smallholding — the capital of a duchy was a farming village with a bath-house.

Every one of those borders was a customs border. Around 1790 the German states had roughly 1,800 customs boundaries. Inside Prussia alone there were over sixty separate local tariffs, and a cargo travelling from Königsberg to Cologne faced something like eighty separate inspections. In 1819 the economist Friedrich List complained that thirty-eight customs and toll lines “paralyse internal traffic”.

Hold on to this. A chemical works needs to buy raw materials cheaply and sell in bulk over a wide area. In 1775 you could do neither. That single fact explains most of what follows.

And if you did want to start a manufacturing business, the guilds stood in the way. Trades were closed shops: who could practise them, where, how many apprentices, what methods. That is a large part of why the chemical trades that did exist were mostly out in the countryside, on streams and in forests, beyond the town walls and the guilds' reach.

Part two

So what chemical industry did exist?

A real one — just not one you would recognise. Across the German states there were hundreds of small works making the handful of chemicals that pre-industrial life actually needed.

The biggest chemical plant you could have visited

It was a salt works, and there were three within reach of Frankfurt: Bad Nauheim (in the Wetterau, about 35 km north of Frankfurt), Bad Soden am Taunus (about 15 km west of Frankfurt, next door to Höchst) and Bad Salzhausen near Nidda (about 50 km north-east). All three are in Hessen — close enough to visit.

The problem at all three was that the brinebrine — a solution of salt in water; here, pumped up from underground. was very dilutedilute — only a little dissolved substance in a lot of water. The opposite of concentrated., and of very low concentrationconcentration — how much dissolved substance there is in a given amount of solution. in the water — barely 3% salt at Nauheim, only 1–1.2% at Soden. Boiling that down with firewood would have been ruinous. So they built graduation towersgraduation tower (Gradierwerk) — a timber frame packed with blackthorn twigs. Brine trickles down it and wind and sun evaporate the water, so what reaches the bottom is more concentrated. (Gradierwerke): huge timber frames packed with blackthorn twigs, over which the brine was pumped and allowed to trickle down again and again, letting wind and sun evaporate the water for free.

Between 1776 and 1786 Johann Wilhelm Langsdorff rebuilt Bad Salzhausen with seven graduation towers, up to 210 metres long, driven by water wheels and pumps. That is the largest piece of chemical engineering a Grade 10 student in this region could have walked up to and touched in 1800 — and its entire purpose was to save fuel.

And at Höchst?

Not a dye works — a porcelain manufactory, founded in 1746, the second oldest in Germany. For its first four years it could only make faience; true porcelain began in 1750, when an Arkanistarcanum — the secret recipe for a porcelain paste and glaze. The Arkanist was the man who kept it: chemical knowledge as private property. arrived. An Arkanist was the keeper of the arcanum, the secret recipe for the paste and the glaze — a chemist in all but name, whose knowledge was so valuable it was a trade secret guarded like treasure.

It went bankrupt in 1796, owing 57,312 gulden. In 1798 the moulds and models were auctioned off. That is what a chemical business looked like at Höchst in our period: a state-privileged luxury workshop, and then a receiver's sale.

A note on the words. The term used in the historical literature is “German Lands” rather than “German States” — from Länder, the same word you meet today in Bundesland. JKW

Sulphuric acid and soda are the two substances on which the whole modern chemical industry is built — and both of them reached the German states after the period this reading covers. Britain had cheap acid in lead chambers while Germany was still distilling it from vitriol in pots.

And the two facts are one fact: the Leblanc process for making soda consumes sulphuric acid. No cheap acid, no soda. No soda, no cheap glass, soap, paper or bleached cloth.

Part three · 1792–1803

Revolution, invasion, and a republic in Mainz

In 1789 France had a revolution. In 1792 the war reached the Rhine. On 21 October 1792 French troops under General Custine entered Mainz without a shot being fired, and the Archbishop-Elector fled.

What happened next was extraordinary: German citizens, led in part by the naturalist and traveller Georg Forster, set up the Mainz Republic — the first attempt at a democratic state on German soil. Elections were held on 24 February 1793, though only 8% of eligible voters turned out. The republic was proclaimed on 18 March 1793.

Part four · 1803–1806

Napoleon redraws the map — and the tolls

On 25 February 1803 came the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss, one of the ugliest words in German history and one of the most consequential documents. At a stroke it abolished the church states and swept away most of the free cities, handing their lands to the surviving princes as compensation for territory lost to France.

For our towns this meant:

  • Höchst and Oberursel stopped being ruled by an archbishop and became part of Nassau-Usingen — in Oberursel's case, a Catholic town now under a Protestant dynasty.
  • Wiesbaden became, in 1806, the capital of the new Duchy of Nassau — a town of about 2,200 people running a country.
  • Frankfurt lost its independence in 1806 and became, from 1810 to 1813, the Grand Duchy of Frankfurt under Karl Theodor von Dalberg.
  • Hesse-Homburg was abolished altogether in 1806 and swallowed by Hesse-Darmstadt.
  • Mainz had already been annexed by France, and from 1800 was the capital of the French Département du Mont-Tonnerre.

Buried in that same document, at §39, is a sentence that matters enormously for the movement of goods:

Source · Reichsdeputationshauptschluss, 25 February 1803, §39

„Alle Rheinzölle werden aufgehoben, blos Eingangs-Gebühren und ein Schiffahrts-Octroi bleiben vorbehalten.“

Translate

“All Rhine tolls are abolished; only entry duties and a navigation octroi are reserved.”

Quoted in Hiram Kümper, „Streit um den Rhein“.

The article, in German (PDF)What the 1803 law did, in English

The following year the Rhine Octroi Convention of 1804 put that into practice. At the end of the eighteenth century there had been about 31 toll stations between Strasbourg and the Dutch border. The convention replaced them with twelve octroi offices on a single uniform tariff, administered from Mainz by what was arguably the world's first international authority.

Careful — a fact that is usually got wrong

You will often read that there were 62 tolls on the Rhine. That figure is real, but it belongs to the end of the fourteenth century, not to 1800. By the 1790s it was about half that. When you meet a striking number, always ask what date it belongs to.

You will also read that Napoleon “freed the Rhine”. Only half true: the 1804 convention cut the tolls, but it confirmed the staple rights and actually revived the boatmen's guilds. Those monopolies survived until 1831.

Part five

Everything moved by water — slowly

There were no railways, and the roads were bad. If you wanted to shift anything heavy — salt, timber, stone, wine, grain, coal — you put it on a river. Which is exactly why, sixty years later, the chemical works were built where they were.

Going downstream was easy: up to 100 river-kilometres a day, just steering. Going upstream was another matter entirely. Boats were towed, from a path along the bank, by horses or by men.

The arithmetic of towing

  • A Rhine boat of about 100 tonnes needed 10 horses in normal water, 12 in low water, and up to 16 when it was very shallow.
  • Where horses could not be used, 60 to 90 men did the work of eight to twelve horses.
  • Upstream progress averaged 25–32 km a day. Downstream, up to 100. A three- to fourfold penalty.
  • Rotterdam to Cologne upstream: up to six weeks.

The Main was worse. Before it was engineered in the nineteenth century it was shallow, winding and braided, with a navigable channel often only half a metre deep. Main boats carried just 15 to 30 tonnes each, against the Rhine's hundred, and larger craft could reach Frankfurt only exceptionally, at high water.

And at Höchst, every cargo going up or down paid the Mainzoll. That toll had been abolished by Emperor Friedrich Barbarossa in 1157, re-imposed, forbidden again, and re-granted — and it was still being collected right through our period. It was finally abolished on 31 December 1866.

The largest moving objects in Europe

The most spectacular thing on the Rhine around 1800 was not a ship. It was a raft — a Holländerfloß, timber from the Black Forest, the Odenwald and the Taunus lashed together and floated down to the Dutch shipyards.

One raft, around 1800

  • Up to 300 metres long and 50 metres wide, drawing about 2 metres
  • A crew of up to 500 men, living aboard in huts
  • Steered by 16–20 oars, each 14 metres long, with seven men to an oar
  • Mainz to Dordrecht in about 12 days, passing 20 toll stations
  • Provisions for one voyage: 40,000 lb of bread, 12,000–20,000 lb of meat, 10,000–15,000 lb of cheese, and 80,000–96,000 litres of beer

On a smaller and more everyday scale, the Marktschiff ran between Mainz and Frankfurt, carrying up to 200 people. Downstream to Mainz it left at ten and arrived by three or four. Coming back upstream it had to be dragged by horses, left at seven in the morning, and got in around three — and cost more, because pulling is dearer than drifting.

Part six · 1806–1814

The Continental System: an accidental experiment in chemistry

By the Berlin Decree of 21 November 1806 Napoleon closed continental Europe to British trade. He could not beat the Royal Navy, so he tried to bankrupt Britain instead. It did not work — but it did something no one intended.

Cut off from colonial imports, continental chemists and manufacturers had to invent substitutessubstitute — a home-made replacement for something you can no longer import.:

  • Beet sugar instead of cane sugar. In 1811 Napoleon decreed beet cultivation on 32,000 hectares and set up schools to teach the process; by 1812 France had 150 beet-sugar factories.
  • Chicory roasted as a coffee substitute — and this was a real industry: in the Magdeburg region in 1797 there were already 14 chicory works employing 1,228 people.
  • Woad revived in place of imported indigo, and chemists hunted for dye substitutes generally.

Saxony's cotton industry grew twentyfold between 1806 and 1813 behind the barrier. But there was a price, and for chemistry it was the whole game: the German states were cut off from British technology at precisely the moment when Britain was perfecting the lead chamber and the Leblanc process.

One man's career, in one paragraph. In 1807 Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner was running the dyeing and bleaching department of a textile works. The blockade cut off the cotton supply and he lost the job. In 1810 the Duke of Weimar called him to Jena as professor of chemistry; in 1812 he was set to building a starch-syrup works, which collapsed when the blockade did. He went on to discover catalysis on platinumcatalyst — speeds a reaction up without being used up, and without changing how much product you finally get. in 1823, to invent a lighter that sold 20,000 units, and in 1829 to publish the law of triads — the first real step towards the periodic table you will meet in Unit 4.

It did not happen. The 1814 peace had said the Rhine should be free jusqu'à la mer — “to the sea”. The Dutch read this as meaning up to the sea, which conveniently left them free to tax the last stretch. Prussia disagreed. Two words held up the Rhine for sixteen years, until the Mainz Convention of 31 March 1831 finally abolished the staple rights and the boatmen's guilds.

Part eight · 27 September 1825

A railway opens in County Durham

On the morning of 27 September 1825, in County Durham, in the north-east of England, a locomotive later named Locomotion No. 1 hauled a train from Shildon through Darlington to Stockton, carrying somewhere between 450 and 600 people on a route of about 42 kilometres.

It was not built for them. The Stockton and Darlington Railway was built for coal — to get it from the collieries to the sea more cheaply than horses and carts could manage. For its first eight years the passenger service was actually subcontracted to stagecoach operators using horses on the rails; the company only ran its own steam passenger trains from 1833.

Nothing changed in Frankfurt that morning. Germany's first steam railway, the six-kilometre line from Nuremberg to Fürth in Bayern, was still ten years away — and from the day after its opening in December 1835, most of its trains were pulled by horses too, with the locomotive Adler taking only the one o'clock and two o'clock departures.

But here is the thing. A railway does not care whether the river is deep enough this month. It does not need a towpath, or ten horses, or six weeks from Rotterdam. It does not stop at Höchst to pay the Mainzoll.

Every constraint described in this reading — shallow water, towing, tolls, guilds, tiny separate states — the railway was about to dissolve. And when it did, the chemical industry that could not exist in 1800 became not just possible but inevitable.

That is where Unit 2 begins: with iron, steel, and the railway explosion.

Questions to think about

  1. In 1800 alum was made by roasting shale and getting less than 2% of it back as product. Explain, using what you know about yield, why alum was expensive — and why that made dyed cloth expensive.
  2. Bad Salzhausen built seven graduation towers, one of them 210 metres long, purely to evaporate water using wind and sun. What were they saving, and why was it worth that much timber and iron to save it?
  3. The Leblanc process for making soda consumes sulphuric acid. Germany had no cheap sulphuric acid until about 1816. Use this to explain why the soda process arrived in Germany over thirty years after it was invented in France.
  4. A cargo went upstream at 25–32 km a day and downstream at up to 100 km a day. Work out the round trip from Mainz to Cologne and back (about 200 km each way). What does that do to the cost of a tonne of salt?
  5. The Continental System both helped and harmed German chemistry. Give one example of each, and say which you think mattered more in the long run.
  6. “There was no chemical industry in the Rhine–Main region in 1800.” Is that statement true, false, or does it depend on what you mean by “industry”? Argue it either way.

Glossary: the chemical words in this reading

Learn these. Most of them come back in every unit of the course, and several are assessed.

WordWhat it meansWhere it appears here
diluteA solution with only a little dissolved substance in a lot of water.Nauheim brine was about 3% salt — very dilute.
concentratedA solution with a lot of dissolved substance in the water. The opposite of dilute.Oleum was absorbed into concentrated sulphuric acid.
concentrationHow much dissolved substance there is in a given amount of solution. Measured later in the course as mol dm−3.The whole point of a graduation tower is to raise the concentration.
solutionA substance dissolved evenly in a liquid.Brine is a solution of salt in water.
brineA solution of salt in water — here, water pumped up from underground.Nauheim, Soden and Salzhausen all worked brine.
saturatedA solution holding as much dissolved substance as it possibly can at that temperature.Brine had to be near saturation before boiling was worth the fuel.
evaporationTurning a liquid into a gas below its boiling point. Uses energy — which is why doing it with wind and sun is free and doing it with firewood is not.Graduation towers evaporate water using wind and sun.
graduation tower
(Gradierwerk)		A timber frame packed with blackthorn twigs. Brine is pumped to the top and trickles down over the twigs; wind and sun evaporate water, so what reaches the bottom is more concentrated.Bad Salzhausen had seven, one 210 m long.
crystallisationForming solid crystals out of a solution, usually by cooling it or evaporating the water.Alum was crystallised in wooden vats.
leachingWashing a soluble substance out of a solid mixture with water.Roasted alum shale was leached to get the alum out.
roasting
(calcining)		Heating a solid strongly in air, to drive off water or gases and change it chemically.Alum shale, sulphide ores and salt cake were all roasted.
retortA closed vessel used for heating a substance and collecting whatever distils off.Iron vitriol was roasted in ceramic retorts to make oleum.
distillationBoiling a liquid and condensing the vapour to separate or purify it.The old vitriol route to sulphuric acid was a distillation.
mordantA substance that fixes a dye to a fibre so it does not wash out. Without one, most dyes simply rinse away.Alum was the mordant — hence over a hundred alum works.
alumPotassium aluminium sulphate. Used as a mordant, in tanning, in paper making and in medicine.Under 2% of the shale came out as alum.
vitriolThe old name for a sulphate salt. Green vitriol = iron(II) sulphate; blue vitriol = copper(II) sulphate.Vitriolöl — oil of vitriol — was sulphuric acid.
oleum
(fuming sulphuric acid)		Sulphur trioxide dissolved in sulphuric acid. Fumes in air. Stronger and more dangerous than ordinary sulphuric acid.Nordhausen oleum, made by the old vitriol route.
lead chamber processThe British method of making sulphuric acid cheaply and in bulk: sulphur dioxide, air, water and nitrogen oxides react inside a large chamber lined with lead — lead being one of the few metals the acid does not eat through.Britain 1746; here, only 1816–1820.
Leblanc processThe French method of making soda from common salt, in two steps: salt + sulphuric acid → salt cake; then salt cake roasted with limestone and coal → soda. It consumes sulphuric acid — which is why the two dates in the table are really one story.Reached here only in 1828.
alkaliA soluble base — the chemical opposite of an acid. Potash and soda are both alkalis.Needed for glass, soap, paper and dyeing.
potashPotassium carbonate, got by burning wood and leaching the ash. The old alkali.80–85% of a glassworks’ wood went on making it.
sodaSodium carbonate. The alkali that replaced potash — cheaper, and not made from forests.The Leblanc process made it from salt.
saltpetrePotassium nitrate. The oxidising ingredient of gunpowder, which is why the state controlled it.Scraped from stables and cellars by licensed Salpetersieder.
pigmentA coloured solid that gives paint its colour, as opposed to a dye, which dissolves and stains.White lead was the main white pigment until c.1835.
tanningTurning raw animal hide into leather using tannins from oak bark, plus lime and sometimes alum. Applied chemistry, done in workshops.Lohmühlen ground the bark for the tanners.
yieldHow much product you actually get, compared with how much you should get in theory. Usually given as a percentage.Alum: under 2%. This decides whether a works makes money.
raw material
(feedstock)		What goes into a process. Its cost and how far it must travel often matter more than the chemistry.Shale, salt, wood, bark, vinegar, manure, brine.
catalystA substance that speeds a reaction up without being used up itself, and without changing how much product you finally get.Döbereiner found hydrogen igniting on platinum in 1823.
substituteA home-made replacement for something you can no longer import.Chicory for coffee; beet for cane sugar; woad for indigo.
arcanum
(and Arkanist)		The secret recipe for a porcelain paste and glaze — and the man who kept it. Chemical knowledge as private property.Höchst could not make porcelain until an Arkanist arrived in 1750.

The one pair students most often confuse

Dilute and concentrated describe how much water you added. They are not the same as weak and strong, which describe how much an acid splits up. You can have a concentrated weak acid and a dilute strong one. You will meet this again in Unit 3, where it is the single most common mistake in the whole topic.

As historians, be on your guard!

Sources are not in agreement, so these are our sources, and here is our best guess!

  1. The population of Oberursel around 1800. Both German and English Wikipedia print “1799: 7,999”. That cannot be right — the same table gives 1,600 for 1618 and only 11,000 for 1959. It looks like a corrupted number, so this reading says “a few thousand” instead. If you can find a reliable figure, bring it.
  2. The date of the first German sulphuric acid works. A doctoral thesis says 1816 at Ringkuhl; an encyclopaedia of 1905 says 1820. This reading gives the range rather than choosing.
  3. The forest-glass figure that 80–85% of a glassworks' wood was burned to make potash rather than to melt glass. It comes from one source and is startlingly high. Treat it as “according to”, not as fact.

Note also that two of the Goethe passages describe events long before they were written down — Dichtung und Wahrheit (1811) remembers the 1750s, and Belagerung von Mainz (1822) recalls 1793. The 1797 Frankfurt passage is the only Goethe here written on the spot. When you use a source, always ask how far it stands from the thing it describes.
