Glossary — quick reference

Relative atomic mass (Ar)
The mass of one atom of an element, compared with 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Read straight off the periodic table.
Molar mass
The mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g/mol) — found by adding the Ar values of every atom in the formula.
Mole
The chemist's "counting unit" for atoms, molecules or formula units. moles = mass ÷ molar mass.
Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound, found from experimental mass data.
Water of crystallisation
Water molecules built into a hydrate's crystal structure in a fixed ratio, shown after a dot, e.g. the "5H₂O" in CuSO₄·5H₂O.
Hydrate
A compound whose crystals include water of crystallisation.
Coefficient
A number placed in front of a whole formula in an equation, showing how many units of it react or form.
Balanced equation
An equation in which the number of atoms of each element is exactly the same on both sides of the arrow.

A. Molar Mass from Formulae

Cheat sheet — Molar Mass from Formulae

Method
1. Write out every atom in the formula, including any hidden by a subscript or bracket. 2. Look up each element's Ar. 3. Multiply each Ar by how many times that atom appears. 4. Add all the totals together — that's the molar mass, in g/mol.
Remember
A subscript after a closing bracket multiplies everything inside the bracket. A coefficient of 1 is never written, and neither is a subscript of 1.
Fresh worked example Molar mass of potassium phosphate, K₃PO₄: 3 potassium (Ar = 39) + 1 phosphorus (Ar = 31) + 4 oxygen (Ar = 16).
  1. Potassium: 3 × 39 = 117
  2. Phosphorus: 1 × 31 = 31
  3. Oxygen: 4 × 16 = 64
  4. Total: 117 + 31 + 64 = 212
Molar mass of K₃PO₄ = 212 g/mol.
Worked example — with a bracket Molar mass of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂. The subscript 2 sits outside the bracket, so it multiplies everything inside it — both the oxygen and the hydrogen — not just the atom nearest the bracket.
  1. Calcium: 1 × 40 = 40
  2. The bracket (OH) contains 1 oxygen and 1 hydrogen. The subscript 2 outside the bracket multiplies both: oxygen now appears 2 times, hydrogen now appears 2 times.
  3. Oxygen: 2 × 16 = 32
  4. Hydrogen: 2 × 1 = 2
  5. Total: 40 + 32 + 2 = 74
Molar mass of Ca(OH)₂ = 74 g/mol.

B. Moles and Mass Conversions

Cheat sheet — Moles and Mass Conversions

The two formulae
moles = mass ÷ molar mass, and (rearranged the other way) mass = moles × molar mass. Same triangle, same three quantities, just read off in whichever direction the question needs.
moles = mass ÷ molar mass   |   mass = moles × molar mass
Fresh worked example A sample contains 4.4 g of carbon dioxide, CO₂ (molar mass = 44 g/mol).
  1. moles = mass ÷ molar mass, so moles = 4.4 ÷ 44 = 0.1 mol
And working the other way: what mass is 0.02 mol of CO₂?
  1. mass = moles × molar mass, so mass = 0.02 × 44 = 0.88 g

C. Formula from Experimental Data

Cheat sheet — Formula from Experimental Data

Method
1. Write down the mass of each element in the sample. 2. Convert each mass to moles (moles = mass ÷ molar mass, which for a single element is just its Ar). 3. Divide every "moles" value by the smallest one, to get the simplest ratio. 4. Those numbers become the subscripts in the formula.
From percentages
Imagine you have exactly 100 g of the compound — then each percentage is simply that many grams, and the method is identical.
Fresh worked example A sample of a compound contains 1.2 g of magnesium and 0.8 g of oxygen.
  1. Moles of Mg = 1.2 ÷ 24 = 0.05
  2. Moles of O = 0.8 ÷ 16 = 0.05
  3. Divide both by the smaller (0.05): Mg = 1, O = 1
Formula = MgO.

D. Calculations Involving Hydrates

Cheat sheet — Calculations Involving Hydrates

Molar mass of a hydrate
Find the Mr of the anhydrous part as normal, then add x × 18 (the molar mass of H₂O) for the water of crystallisation.
Finding x from heating data
1. Mass of water lost = mass of hydrate − mass of anhydrous salt (after heating to constant mass). 2. Moles of water = mass of water ÷ 18. 3. Moles of anhydrous salt = mass of anhydrous salt ÷ its molar mass. 4. x = moles of water ÷ moles of anhydrous salt.
Fresh worked example A student heats 25.0 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, to constant mass. 16.0 g of anhydrous CuSO₄ (Mr = 160) remains.
  1. Mass of water lost = 25.0 − 16.0 = 9.0 g
  2. Moles of water = 9.0 ÷ 18 = 0.5 mol
  3. Moles of CuSO₄ = 16.0 ÷ 160 = 0.1 mol
  4. x = 0.5 ÷ 0.1 = 5
Formula = CuSO₄·5H₂O.

E. Writing and Balancing Equations

Cheat sheet — Writing and Balancing Equations

Method
Count the atoms of each element on both sides. If one doesn't match, place a coefficient in front of a formula — never change a subscript — then recount everything, since fixing one element can unbalance another. Keep going until every element matches. A coefficient of 1 is never written.
Remember
A coefficient sits outside and in front of a whole formula, multiplying it as a unit. A subscript is fixed as part of the formula itself — changing one makes a different substance entirely.
Fresh worked example Aluminium reacts with oxygen: Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃.
  1. Count: left has 1 Al, 2 O. Right has 2 Al, 3 O. Nothing matches yet.
  2. Oxygen needs a multiple of both 2 and 3 — that's 6. Al₂O₃ × 2 gives 6 oxygens: Al + O₂ → 2Al₂O₃. O₂ × 3 also gives 6: Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃.
  3. Recount aluminium: the right side now has 4 (2 × 2). Al × 4 fixes it: 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃.
Balanced equation = 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃.
