Chunk 2. What the words and letters mean.

mass, m = mass in g. molar mass, M = molar mass in g mol−1. number of moles, n = moles in mol.

Chunk 3. Method: one column for each element.

With percentages, say “Let’s take 100 g of the compound”. Then 36% is 36 g.

number of moles, n = mass, mmolar mass, M

Divide every number of moles by the smallest. If you get about 1.5, multiply by 2. If you get about 1.33, multiply by 3.

Reflect: a number of moles less than one is sensible when the mass is less than the molar mass. If it looks wrong, check the calculator: the smaller number is the numerator.

System, surroundings and conservation of energy

[6 marks]

Cheat sheet: system, surroundings and the Law of Conservation of Energy

Chunk 1. Definitions.

The system is the chemicals that are reacting. The surroundings are everything else: the water, the cup or tube, the thermometer and the air.

Chunk 2. Which way does the energy go?

TypeTemperature of the surroundingsEnergy moves
exothermicrisesfrom the system to the surroundings
endothermicfallsfrom the surroundings to the system

Chunk 3. Law of Conservation of Energy.

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It is only transferred. The energy lost by one part is equal to the energy gained by the other part, so the total energy does not change.

New words in this part
system
the chemicals that are reacting.
surroundings
everything around the chemicals: the cup, thermometer, air and any water that is not reacting.
exothermic
a reaction that gives out energy, so the surroundings get warmer.
endothermic
a reaction that takes in energy, so the surroundings get cooler.

– is a single bond, = a double bond, ≡ a triple bond.

Chunk 2. Method.

Breaking bonds takes energy in. Making bonds gives energy out. Count each bond, multiply by its energy, and add.

enthalpy change, ΔH = (total energy to break bonds) − (total energy given out making bonds)

More energy out than in: exothermic, enthalpy change negative. More energy in than out: endothermic, enthalpy change positive.

New words in this part
bond enthalpy
the energy needed to break one mole of a bond, in kJ mol−1.
enthalpy change, ΔH
the energy change of a reaction. Negative for an exothermic reaction and positive for an endothermic reaction.
Worked example: hydrogen and bromine

H2(g) + Br2(g) → 2HBr(g)

H–H  +  Br–Br  →  2 × H–Br

Energy taken in: 436 + 193 = 629 kJ. Energy given out: 2 × 366 = 732 kJ.

enthalpy change, ΔH = 629 − 732 = −103 kJ mol−1, so the reaction is exothermic.

Calorimetry

[7 marks]

Cheat sheet: calorimetry

Chunk 1. Formulae.

local energy change, q = total volume × 4.2 × Δtemperature

number of moles, n = concentration × volume1000

enthalpy change, ΔH = local energy change, q, in kJnumber of moles, n

total volume = the volume of the whole mixture in cm3. Δtemperature = the size of the temperature change in °C (no sign). The local energy change, q, is in J: to change to kJ, J1000. In the moles formula, volume is the volume of the limiting reactant in cm3 and concentration is in mol dm−3.

Chunk 2. The sign goes on the enthalpy change, ΔH, last.

TemperatureTypeΔH
risesexothermicnegative

New words in this part
calorimetry
measuring the energy of a reaction from the temperature change it causes in water.
limiting reactant
the reactant that is used up first. It decides how much product is made and how many moles to use in the calculation.
in excess
more than enough, so some is left over at the end. The reactant in excess is not used for the moles.
local energy change, q
the energy gained by the solution, in J.
Worked example: hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide solution

25 cm3 of 2.00 mol dm−3 hydrochloric acid is mixed with 25 cm3 of an excess of sodium hydroxide solution (CARE: CORROSIVE!). The temperature rises from 19.0 °C to 32.5 °C. The acid is the limiting reactant.

  • Total volume of the mixture = 25 + 25 = 50 cm3
  • Δtemperature = 32.5 − 19.0 = 13.5 °C
  • Local energy change, q = 50 × 4.2 × 13.5 = 2835 J, and 28351000 = 2.835 kJ
  • Number of moles of acid, n = 2.00 × 251000 = 0.0500 mol
  • Enthalpy change, ΔH = 2.8350.0500 = 56.7, and the temperature rose, so ΔH = −56.7 kJ mol−1

Brownian motion, the Kinetic Theory and the Collision Theory

[8 marks]

Cheat sheet: Brownian motion, the Kinetic Theory and the Collision Theory

Chunk 1. Brownian motion.

Brownian motion is the random zigzag movement of tiny visible specks, such as pollen in water or smoke in air. It happens because fast-moving, invisible particles keep hitting the speck, and at any moment the hits are not balanced. It is our evidence that matter is made of particles that are always moving. The hotter the substance, the faster its particles move, so the specks jiggle more.

Chunk 2. The Kinetic Theory (the Particle Theory).

StateHow close?How do the particles move?
solidtouching, in regular rowsvibrate about fixed positions
liquidtouching, no patternslide past each other
gasfar apartmove fast in random directions

Chunk 3. The Collision Theory.

A collision is successful only if the particles (1) collide, (2) hit with at least the activation energy (a long arrow: fast enough) and (3) have the right orientation (the black spots face each other). The rate of reaction is the number of successful collisions each second.

New words in this part
Brownian motion
the random zigzag movement of visible specks, caused by collisions with invisible fast-moving particles.
speck
a tiny grain, such as smoke or pollen, big enough to see through a microscope.
random
with no pattern: any direction is possible.
vibrate
move to and fro about a fixed position.
activation energy
the minimum energy that colliding particles need for the collision to be successful.
orientation
the way round that the particles face as they collide.
