Activity 3: Formula from Experimental Data — Finding the Empirical Formula

Turning real measurements — the mass of each element in a sample — into the formula of the compound they came from. This is the reverse of everything you've practised so far: instead of starting with a formula and finding its molar mass, you start with masses and work out the formula. Lots of examples, starting easy and building up gradually. Self-marking, with hints, and a bit of extra practice folded in automatically if a question doesn't go your way.

Glossary

Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. This is what you calculate on this page.
Molecular formula
The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of a compound — sometimes the same as the empirical formula, sometimes a whole-number multiple of it (you'll meet this properly later).
Percentage composition by mass
What percentage of a compound's total mass comes from each element. Treating it as a mass out of exactly 100 g uses exactly the same method as any other mass data.
Simplest whole-number ratio
The smallest set of whole numbers that has the same proportions as the numbers you started with — found by dividing every value by the smallest one.
Relative atomic mass (Ar)
The mass of one atom of an element, compared with 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Read straight off the periodic table — for a single element, this is also its molar mass, which is what moles = mass ÷ molar mass actually divides by.

B. The new method new idea

Four steps, every time:

  1. Write down the mass of each element in the sample.
  2. Convert each mass to moles (moles = mass ÷ molar mass, which for a single element is just its Ar).
  3. Divide every one of those "moles" numbers by the smallest one, to get the simplest ratio.
  4. Those numbers become the subscripts in the formula.
Worked example A sample of a compound is found to contain 0.2 g of hydrogen and 1.6 g of oxygen.
  1. Moles of H = 0.2 ÷ 1 = 0.2
  2. Moles of O = 1.6 ÷ 16 = 0.1
  3. Divide both by the smaller value (0.1): H = 0.2 ÷ 0.1 = 2, O = 0.1 ÷ 0.1 = 1
  4. Ratio H : O = 2 : 1
Formula = H₂O.

4. In step 3 of the method, which value do you divide everything by?

HintLook at the worked example above — which number was 0.1, and what happened to it?
Model answer (advanced)Dividing by the smallest value always turns that element into exactly 1 in the ratio, which is what makes every other element's number easy to read off.

F. Starting from percentages new idea

Data is sometimes given as percentage composition by mass rather than actual grams. The trick: imagine you have exactly 100 g of the compound — then each percentage is simply that many grams, and the method is identical to before.

Worked example A compound is found to contain, by mass, 60% magnesium and 40% oxygen.
  1. Imagine 100 g of the compound: 60 g Mg and 40 g O.
  2. Moles of Mg = 60 ÷ 24 = 2.5. Moles of O = 40 ÷ 16 = 2.5.
  3. Divide both by the smaller (2.5): 1 : 1.
Formula = MgO.

G. Spot the mistake diagnostic

Each question shows a calculation someone actually got wrong. Work out what went wrong before you answer.

19. A student has 2.4 g of magnesium and 1.6 g of oxygen. They write the ratio straight from the masses, 2.4 : 1.6, simplify it to 3 : 2, and give the formula as Mg₃O₂. What did they forget to do?

HintA ratio of masses is not the same thing as a ratio of moles — atoms of different elements don't weigh the same.
Model answer (advanced)Mass ratios and mole ratios are only ever the same by coincidence — Mg atoms are 24 times heavier than 1 g, O atoms 16 times, so the mass ratio and the true atom ratio are quite different here.

20. Using the correct method, what is the actual formula of the compound in question 19?

HintYou've seen this exact pair of masses already — look back at question 5.
Model answer (advanced)MgO — Mg₃O₂ from question 19 isn't just imprecise, it's a different, non-existent compound.

21. A student correctly finds moles of C = 0.1 and moles of O = 0.2, but then divides both by the larger value (0.2) instead of the smaller one, getting 0.5 : 1. What should they have done instead?

HintLook back at step 3 of the method in Section B.
Model answer (advanced)Dividing by the larger value gives a fraction for the smaller element (0.5), which is much harder to read as a whole-number ratio than dividing by the smaller value, which always turns that element into a clean 1.

22. What is the correct simplest whole-number ratio, C : O, from question 21? (Write it as two numbers separated by a colon, e.g. "1:2".)

HintDivide 0.1 and 0.2 by the smaller value, 0.1.
Model answer (advanced)1:2 — this is the same C and O data as question 9, giving the formula CO₂.

Bonus round stretch

A harder case where the ratio doesn't come out as whole numbers straight away, and a first look at the difference between an empirical and a molecular formula.

Worked example — when dividing down isn't quite enough A sample of a compound contains 11.2 g of iron and 4.8 g of oxygen.
  1. Moles of Fe = 11.2 ÷ 56 = 0.2. Moles of O = 4.8 ÷ 16 = 0.3.
  2. Divide both by the smaller (0.2): Fe = 1, O = 1.5.
  3. 1.5 isn't a whole number — so multiply both numbers by 2: Fe = 2, O = 3.
Formula = Fe₂O₃. This "multiply up" trick is the regular territory of the next activity in this series.

26. Using the worked example above, what is the formula of the compound?

HintIt's worked out for you just above.
Model answer (advanced)Fe₂O₃ — iron(III) oxide, the compound in rust.

27. A compound's empirical formula is CH₂ (the CH₂ unit has a mass of 14 g/mol). Its actual molar mass is 42 g/mol. Since 42 ÷ 14 = 3, the molecular formula must be 3 times the empirical formula. What is the molecular formula?

HintMultiply every subscript in CH₂ by 3.
Model answer (advanced)C₃H₆ — this is exactly the extra step that turns an empirical formula into a molecular formula, and it's why the two aren't always the same thing.
