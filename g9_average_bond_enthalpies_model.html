Average bond enthalpies: energy from alcohols

Learning objectives

  • Balance the equation for the complete combustion of an alcohol.
  • Build the molecules, count the bonds in each and identify their bond angles.
  • Count the bonds broken and the bonds formed, and use average bond enthalpies to calculate ΔH.
  • Scale ΔH to one mole of alcohol, then convert it to energy released per gram, and per cm³.
  • Recognise that bond enthalpies describe gases, and correct for the alcohol and the water being liquids.

Hint: choosing the angle

Count the electron pairs around the central atom: the bonding pairs (a double bond counts as one region) and the lone pairs. The pairs repel and spread as far apart as they can. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so they squeeze the bond angle smaller.

  • 2 regions, no lone pairs: the bonds point in opposite directions.
  • 4 regions, no lone pairs: the bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron.
  • 4 regions, two of them lone pairs: the angle is squeezed below the tetrahedral value.

Now count the bonds of each type in one molecule, reading them from the molecule you built. Enter 0 where a bond type is absent.

Step 8 therefore combined an energy for a gas with a density for a liquid. The density has to be that of the liquid, because a liquid fuel holds about a thousand times more alcohol in each cm³ than its vapour does. The energy has to be corrected instead.

Correction 1: vaporising the alcohol

Before liquid alcohol can burn as a gas, each mole must be turned into a gas. That takes in energy, called the enthalpy of vaporisation. Burning the liquid will therefore release energy than burning the gas.

energy released (liquid alcohol) = energy released (gas alcohol) − energy to vaporise
energy released = – − – = kJ mol⁻¹

Use the size of each energy, with no signs. Round the energy from Step 5 to the nearest whole number.

energy released (J g⁻¹) = energy released (kJ mol⁻¹) × 1000 ÷ Mr
energy released = – × 1000 ÷ – = – J g⁻¹
energy density (J cm⁻³) = energy released (J g⁻¹) × density (g cm⁻³)
energy density = – × – = – J cm⁻³

Correction 2: condensing the water

Bond enthalpies give the water as a gas. Cooled to room temperature, as in a calorimeter, the water condenses to a liquid and releases energy: 44 kJ for every mole of water. (In a flame or an engine the water leaves as steam, and this energy is not collected.)

From your balanced equation, burning 1 mole of alcohol makes mole(s) of water.

energy released (liquid alcohol, liquid water) = energy released (Correction 1) + 44 × moles of water
energy released = – + 44 × – = kJ mol⁻¹
energy released = – × 1000 ÷ – = – J g⁻¹
energy density = – × – = – J cm⁻³ = – kJ cm⁻³

Underlying ideas: definitions to learn

  • Average bond enthalpy: the mean energy needed to break one mole of a bond, with the substances in the gas state.
  • Breaking bonds takes in energy (endothermic). Making bonds gives out energy (exothermic).
  • ΔH = energy in (bonds broken) − energy out (bonds formed). A negative ΔH means the reaction is exothermic.
  • Enthalpy of vaporisation: the energy taken in to turn one mole of a liquid into a gas. Bond enthalpies describe gases, so a liquid fuel needs this correction.
  • Condensing releases energy: 44 kJ for each mole of water vapour that becomes liquid water.
  • Molar mass (Mr for a compound) links moles to grams.
  • Density: mass ÷ volume, in g cm⁻³.
