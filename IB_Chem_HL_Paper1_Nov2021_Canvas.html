Question 1 Topic 1 — Stoichiometry
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • Avogadro's constant L (or NA) converts a number of particles into a number of moles: moles = particles ÷ L.
  • Work out how many atoms of the element you care about are in one molecule of the compound before you touch the arithmetic.
  • Ethanol is C₂H₅OH — count the carbons in the formula, not the hydrogens or oxygen.

How much ethanol contains 1.20 × 10²⁴ atoms of carbon?

Avogadro's constant, L or NA: 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

Hint 1First find the moles of carbon atoms: divide the atom count by L.
Hint 2That gives you moles of carbon atoms, not moles of ethanol. Each ethanol molecule (C₂H₅OH) contains 2 carbon atoms, so divide your answer by 2 to get moles of ethanol.
Question 2 Topic 1 — Stoichiometry
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • Use the balanced equation's mole ratios to work out how much of each reactant is actually used up.
  • The reagent that runs out first is the limiting reagent; everything else is present in excess, and some of it survives unreacted.
  • Read the question carefully — it asks what remains at the end, not what you started with.

3.00 mol of C₃H₈ is mixed with 20.00 mol of O₂. Which quantity is present at the end of the reaction?

C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g)

Hint 1For every 1 mol of C₃H₈ that reacts, 5 mol of O₂ is used up. How much O₂ is needed to react with all 3.00 mol of C₃H₈?
Hint 23.00 mol C₃H₈ requires 3.00 × 5 = 15.00 mol O₂. You started with 20.00 mol O₂, so C₃H₈ is the limiting reagent and is entirely consumed — work out what's left of the O₂.
Question 3 Topic 1 — Stoichiometry (gases)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • When two gas samples at the same temperature are mixed, total pressure follows a weighted average based on volume — this comes from PV = constant for each portion, combined at constant total volume.
  • Useful shortcut: Ptotal = (P₁V₁ + P₂V₂) / (V₁ + V₂), where V₁ + V₂ is the new combined volume once the valve opens.

The two containers shown are connected by a valve. What is the total pressure after the valve is opened and the two gas samples are allowed to mix at constant temperature?

Hint 1Think in terms of moles: n ∝ PV at constant T (Boyle's law form). Work out P₁V₁ and P₂V₂ for each container separately.
Hint 2Add P₁V₁ + P₂V₂, then divide by the new total volume (V₁ + V₂ = 6.0 dm³).
Question 4 Topic 2 — Atomic structure
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • Neutrons = mass number − atomic number (proton number).
  • Electrons = atomic number − charge (for a cation, subtract the positive charge; for an anion, add the negative charge's magnitude).
  • Work through all four species systematically rather than trying to spot the answer — it's easy to make a sign error.

Which species has two more neutrons than electrons?

⁶₃Li⁺    ⁹₄Be²⁺    ²³₁₁Na⁺    ⁴²₂₀Ca²⁺

Hint 1For each species, find neutrons (mass − Z) and electrons (Z − charge) separately, then subtract.
Hint 2Na: Z = 11, mass = 23, so neutrons = 12; electrons = 11 − 1 = 10. Compare 12 − 10 to the other three species.
Question 5 Topic 2/12 — Atomic structure (ionisation energy)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • Write out the electron configurations before and after each ionisation — comparing sub-shells (3s vs 3p) is where most marks are lost.
  • First IE of Mg removes an electron from 3s² (Mg → Mg⁺, [Ne]3s¹). Second IE of Al removes an electron from Al⁺'s configuration, [Ne]3s² (Al⁺ → Al²⁺).
  • If two ionisations remove an electron from the same type of orbital and leave the same number of electrons behind, the difference in energy comes down to nuclear charge (proton number) alone.

Which statement explains why the second ionization energy of aluminium is higher than the first ionization energy of magnesium?

Hint 1Al's first IE removes the single 3p electron, leaving Al⁺ as [Ne]3s² — the same electron count and arrangement as an Mg atom about to lose its first electron.
Hint 2Since both processes remove an electron from an isoelectronic [Ne]3s² arrangement, only the nuclear charge differs between the two species — which one has more protons?
Question 14 Topic 5 — Energetics (bond enthalpies)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • Energy released ≈ (energy of bonds formed) − (energy of bonds broken), using the bond enthalpies given.
  • The C–H and C–C bonds in C₃H₈, and the O–H bonds in H₂O, are identical across all four options, so they cancel when you compare — focus only on what differs: the O=O bonds broken and the C=O / C≡O bonds formed.
  • Incomplete combustion (forming CO rather than CO₂) uses less O₂ but forms very strong C≡O bonds (1000 kJ mol⁻¹) — don't assume incomplete combustion always releases less energy.

Which combustion reaction releases the least energy per mole of C₃H₈?

Approximate bond enthalpy / kJ mol⁻¹
O=O   500    C=O   800    C≡O   1000

Hint 1For each option, total the C=O and C≡O bonds formed (2 × 800 per CO₂, 1 × 1000 per CO) and the O=O bonds broken (500 × mol of O₂).
Hint 2Net energy released ≈ bonds formed − bonds broken. Calculate this for all four and compare — the smallest value is the answer.

Hint 1Rule out any equation where the product's coefficient isn't 1, and any equation where an element isn't in its standard state.

Question 16 Topic 5/15 — Energetics (Born–Haber cycle, HL)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • A Born–Haber cycle is Hess's law applied to lattice enthalpy: the direct route (ΔHf) must equal the sum of the indirect route's steps.
  • Go around the cycle in one consistent direction, adding each step's enthalpy change with its correct sign as printed on the diagram.
  • The lattice enthalpy step is usually the one 'missing' arrow you're asked to find — set up the equation ΔHf = (sum of all other steps) + (lattice enthalpy term), then rearrange.

Consider the Born–Haber cycle for the formation of sodium oxide. What is the lattice enthalpy, in kJ mol⁻¹, of sodium oxide?

Hint 1Write the Hess's law equation: −414 = 2(108) + 249 + 2(496) + (−141) + 790 + (−LE), where −LE is the step going from the gaseous ions down to the solid.
Hint 2Rearrange that equation to make LE (lattice enthalpy) the subject — watch the sign changes carefully as you move each term across the equals sign.
Question 17 Topic 15/17 — Energetics (Gibbs free energy, HL)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • Spontaneity of a reaction is governed by ΔG: ΔG < 0 means spontaneous in the forward direction.
  • ΔG⊖ = −RT ln K, so K > 1 under standard conditions corresponds to ΔG⊖ < 0 — spontaneous.
  • A negative cell potential means ΔG > 0 (since ΔG = −nFE); a negative ΔSuniverse is the signature of a non-spontaneous process (2nd law); and ΔG of the reverse reaction being negative means ΔG of the forward reaction is positive.

In which of the following situations is the forward reaction spontaneous?

Hint 1Translate each option into a statement about ΔG for the forward reaction — you're looking for the one where ΔG < 0.
Hint 2Remember ΔG⊖ = −RT ln K: what does K > 1 do to ln K, and hence to ΔG⊖?

Question 21 Topic 16 — Kinetics (mechanism, HL)

  • The rate equation is determined only by the species involved in (and before) the slow, rate-determining step.
  • If an intermediate I forms in a fast pre-equilibrium step, its concentration can be treated as proportional to the species that formed it, and substituted into the rate expression for the slow step.
  • Check that the overall rate expression you derive for each mechanism actually simplifies to rate = k[A][B].

rate = k[A][B]

Which mechanism is consistent with this rate equation?

Question 22 Topic 7 — Equilibrium
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • Compare the reaction quotient Q to the equilibrium constant Kc to predict which way a reaction will shift.
  • If Q < K, the system has 'too much reactant' relative to equilibrium, so the forward reaction proceeds faster than the reverse until Q rises to meet K.
  • If Q > K, the reverse reaction dominates instead; if Q = K, the system is already at equilibrium.

A reversible reaction has a reaction quotient, Q, of 4.5 and equilibrium constant, Kc, of 6.2.

2A(g) ⇌ A₂(g)

Which statement describes the reaction at this time?

Hint 1Since Q ≠ K, the system is not yet at equilibrium — that rules out one option immediately.
Hint 2Is Q smaller or larger than K here? Which direction does the system need to shift to make Q increase toward K?
Question 23 Topic 7/17 — Equilibrium (Gibbs free energy, HL)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • At constant temperature and pressure, a reacting system moves toward the composition of minimum Gibbs free energy.
  • The equilibrium point on a G vs composition graph is therefore the lowest point of the curve, not the midpoint of the x-axis.
  • Don't assume equilibrium is where the amounts of reactant and product happen to be equal — read the position of the minimum directly off the graph.

The graph shows Gibbs free energy of a mixture of N₂O₄(g) and NO₂(g) in different proportions. Which point shows the system at equilibrium?

N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g)

Hint 1Equilibrium corresponds to the turning point (minimum) of the Gibbs free energy curve.
Hint 2Scan the curve from N₂O₄ (left) to NO₂ (right) and find where G stops falling and starts rising again.

Question 25 Topic 8 — Acids and bases (pH)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • KOH is a strong base that dissociates completely, so [OH⁻] = the given concentration of KOH.
  • pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻], and pH + pOH = 14 (at 25 °C, Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴).
  • Powers of ten and logs like this are exactly what the paper expects you to handle without a calculator — 0.01 = 10⁻², so log(0.01) is exact.

What is the pH of 0.01 mol dm⁻³ KOH(aq)?

Hint 1Find pOH first: pOH = −log(0.01).
Hint 2Then use pH = 14 − pOH.
Question 26 Topic 8/18 — Acids and bases (titration curves, HL)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • At the equivalence point of a titration, moles of acid = moles of base (stoichiometrically), but the pH there depends on the strength of the acid and base, not just the amounts.
  • Strong acid + weak base → the salt formed contains a weakly acidic conjugate (e.g. NH₄⁺), so the equivalence point pH is below 7 (acidic).
  • Contrast this with strong acid + strong base (equivalence at pH 7) and weak acid + strong base (equivalence above pH 7).

What is a possible value of pH at the equivalence point in the titration of a strong acid with a weak base?

Hint 1Think about what salt forms at equivalence, and whether its ions react with water (hydrolyse) to make the solution acidic, neutral, or basic.
Hint 2A weak base's conjugate acid (e.g. NH₄⁺ from NH₃) is itself a weak acid — what does that do to the pH of the resulting solution?
Question 27 Topic 8 — Acids and bases (K_w)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • The autoionisation of water, H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻, is endothermic, so Kw increases as temperature rises.
  • Pure water is always neutral in the sense that [H⁺] = [OH⁻] — that equality doesn't depend on temperature.
  • 'Neutral' (equal ion concentrations) and 'pH = 7' are only the same thing at 25 °C, where Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴. At higher temperature, Kw is larger, so neutral pH is lower than 7.

What is correct for pure hot water?

Hint 1Pure water always has [H⁺] = [OH⁻], whatever the temperature — this rules out two of the four options immediately.
Hint 2As temperature rises, does Kw (and hence [H⁺]) increase or decrease? What happens to pH as [H⁺] increases?

Question 29 Topic 9 — Redox (voltaic cells)
Cheat sheet — what you need before you start
  • In a voltaic cell, the more reactive metal is oxidised at the anode and loses electrons; those electrons travel through the external wire to the cathode.
  • Electrons in the wire always flow from the anode (oxidation, more reactive metal) to the cathode (reduction, less reactive metal) — never through the solution.
  • In the salt bridge, anions (negative ions) migrate toward the half-cell that is building up positive charge — i.e., toward the anode, where the metal is dissolving into solution as cations.

Consider this voltaic cell, where Cu is a more reactive metal than Ag. Which combination describes the movement of charge in this cell?

Hint 1Since Cu is more reactive, it is oxidised (loses electrons) at its electrode. Which direction does that send the electrons in the external wire — toward or away from the Cu electrode?
Hint 2As Cu²⁺ builds up in the copper half-cell, that side becomes more positively charged. Which ions in the salt bridge move to balance that charge — the negative or positive ones — and in which direction?

  • Units of a concentration/time gradient are mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ (concentration units divided by time units) — check the answer options carefully, since some deliberately drop the 's⁻¹'.

What is the slope of the graph?

Glossary

Limiting reagent
The reactant that is completely used up first, stopping the reaction and determining the maximum amount of product formed.
Avogadro's constant (L, N_A)
The number of particles (atoms, molecules or ions) in one mole of a substance: 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
Ionisation energy
The energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms or ions.
Standard enthalpy of formation (ΔH_f⊖)
The enthalpy change when exactly one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions.
Lattice enthalpy
The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid is formed from its gaseous ions (or, for the reverse process, broken apart into them).
Born–Haber cycle
An application of Hess's law that relates the enthalpy of formation of an ionic compound to the sum of the individual steps (atomisation, ionisation, electron affinity, lattice enthalpy) that build it up.
Gibbs free energy (G)
A thermodynamic quantity combining enthalpy and entropy; a process is spontaneous at constant temperature and pressure when ΔG is negative.
Reaction quotient (Q)
A measure of the relative amounts of products and reactants at any point during a reaction, calculated the same way as K but not necessarily at equilibrium.
Rate-determining step
The slowest step in a reaction mechanism, which controls the overall rate and determines the form of the experimental rate equation.
Hydrolysis
A reaction in which a species reacts with water, often producing H⁺ or OH⁻ and shifting the pH of the solution.
Equivalence point
The point in a titration at which the amount of titrant added is stoichiometrically equivalent to the amount of the substance being titrated.
K_w (ionic product of water)
The equilibrium constant for the autoionisation of water, H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻; equal to 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C, and larger at higher temperatures.
Oxidation state
A number assigned to an atom that represents the charge it would have if all bonds to different atoms were fully ionic.
Anode / cathode
In a voltaic cell, the anode is where oxidation occurs (electrons are released); the cathode is where reduction occurs (electrons are gained).
Salt bridge
A connection between the two half-cells of a voltaic cell that allows ion flow to balance charge, without allowing the solutions to mix freely.
Standard electrode potential (E⊖)
A measure of the tendency of a half-cell to be reduced, measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions.
Inert electrode
An electrode (e.g. platinum or graphite) that conducts electricity but does not itself take part in the electrode reaction.
