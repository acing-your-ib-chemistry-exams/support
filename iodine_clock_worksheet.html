The iodine clock reaction: rate, concentration and graphs

You will take the Royal Society of Chemistry iodine clock recipe (solutions A and B) and turn it into numbers: the concentrations in the beaker, the rate of the reaction, and the graphs that show how rate depends on concentration.

Safety

  • Wear eye protection throughout.
  • Solution B contains hydrogen peroxide (an irritant) and ethanoic acid. Solution A contains thiosulfate ions. Keep A and B apart until you are ready to start the clock. Acid mixed with thiosulfate ions slowly gives toxic sulfur dioxide and a sulfur precipitate, so never leave a mixture standing.
  • Measure with the syringes your teacher gives you, mix only the volumes in the tables, and dispose of every mixture as your teacher directs.
  • Wash your hands after the practical and wipe up spills straight away.

Learning objectives and key ideas

By the end of this worksheet you should be able to:

  • explain, using two equations, why the mixture stays colourless and then turns blue-black suddenly;
  • turn the masses and volumes in the recipe into concentrations in the mixture;
  • calculate a rate from a clock time, and explain why it is an initial rate;
  • plot concentration–time and rate–concentration graphs, and use them to find an order and a rate constant.

Two reactions share the iodine

The slow reaction makes iodine. This is the reaction whose rate we measure:

H2O2(aq) + 2I−(aq) + 2H+(aq) → I2(aq) + 2H2O(l)

The fast reaction removes the iodine again as soon as it forms, using thiosulfate ions from solution A:

2S2O32−(aq) + I2(aq) → S4O62−(aq) + 2I−(aq)

Only when every thiosulfate ion has gone does iodine survive in solution. Iodine, with the excess iodide ions present, forms a blue-black complex with starch. The stopwatch is therefore the thiosulfate: a fixed amount of it takes a time t to use up.

Underlying ideas: definitions to learn

rate of reactionthe change in concentration of a reactant or product in unit time. Units: mol dm−3 s−1.
initial ratethe rate at the start of the reaction, when the concentrations are the ones you prepared.
rate equationan equation linking the rate to the concentrations of reactants, found only by experiment.
order of reactionfor one reactant, the power to which its concentration is raised in the rate equation. It cannot be read from the balanced equation.
rate constant, kthe constant in the rate equation. It changes with temperature.
rate-determining stepthe slowest step in a reaction mechanism; it sets the overall rate.
clock reactiona reaction in which a sudden change shows when a fixed amount of reaction has happened, so a time is converted to a rate.
buffera solution that resists changes in pH; here ethanoic acid with sodium ethanoate.

The overall equation for the slow reaction contains 2H+, yet the rate equation above has no [H+] term. In this buffered mixture [H+] is effectively constant, so any effect of it is included in the value of k; your k applies to this recipe at this temperature, not to every solution.
