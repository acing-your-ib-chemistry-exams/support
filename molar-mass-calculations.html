Relative formula mass

Sodium chloride is written NaCl, but it isn't really a single molecule — it's a giant lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Strictly, a compound like this has no "molecular" mass, because it has no individual molecules. What it does have is a relative formula mass: the relative atomic masses of everything in the formula, added together. For NaCl that's sodium (23) plus chlorine (35.5), giving 58.5.

Relative atomic mass, relative molecular mass, and relative formula mass are all ratios compared with carbon-12 — none of them carry units.

Molar mass

In the lab you weigh things in grams, not in "relative mass units". Molar mass takes the same number as the relative formula mass and gives it units of grams per mole, g mol⁻¹. You find it the same way: add up the relative atomic mass of every atom shown in the formula.

The two most common slips: missing a multiplier that applies to a whole bracketed group — Pb(NO₃)₂ has two nitrate groups, so six oxygens, not three — and miscounting a water of crystallisation, where CuSO₄·5H₂O has 5 × 2 = 10 hydrogens and 5 oxygens from the water, on top of the four already in the sulfate.
