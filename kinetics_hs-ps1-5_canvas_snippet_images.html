Kinetics: rates of reactions

Learning objectives and cheat sheets

Grade 9 Chemistry · Unit 3 · NGSS HS-PS1-5

What this unit asks of us

By the end of the unit we can use evidence to explain how changing the temperature or the concentration of the reacting particles changes the rate of a reaction. We give the explanation in terms of collisions between particles, and we use the same ideas to explain what a catalyst does.

1  What is rate, and how do we measure it?

Learning objectives

☐  I can define the rate of a reaction as the change in concentration divided by the change in time.

☐  I can give the units of rate and calculate a rate from a change in concentration and the time taken.

☐  I can explain why a rate is always a positive number.

☐  I can describe how to follow a reaction by timing a fixed change, or by measuring a gas volume, a mass or a colour against time.

☐  I can explain why a concentration–time graph is steep at the start and flattens later, and read a rate from its slope.

Cheat sheet

Definition. The rate of a reaction is how quickly the concentration of a reactant falls, or of a product rises.

rate = 
Δc
Δt

Δc is the change in concentration (mol dm−3). Δt is the time taken for that change (s). So the unit of rate is mol dm−3 s−1: concentration change per second.

Signs. A reactant’s concentration falls and a product’s concentration rises. We always take Δc and Δt as positive sizes (larger value take away smaller value), so a rate is never negative.

Worked example

A reactant falls from 0.80 mol dm−3 to 0.50 mol dm−3 in 60 s. Find the rate.

Step 1. Find Δc (larger take away smaller).

Δc = 0.80 − 0.50 = 0.30 mol dm−3

Step 2. Write the formula, then the numbers, then calculate.

rate = 
Δc
Δt
rate = 
0.30
60
 = 0.0050 mol dm−3 s−1

Common errors

  • A rate compares a change in concentration with time. The total amount of product made is a different question.
  • A rate needs “per second”. mol dm−3 on its own is a concentration, not a rate.
  • A rate is never negative. Take Δc and Δt as positive sizes.

2  What must happen for particles to react?

Learning objectives

☐  I can state that particles must collide before they can react.

☐  I can explain why most collisions do not lead to a reaction: too little energy, or the wrong orientation.

☐  I can define activation energy, Ea.

☐  I can draw and label an energy profile for an exothermic and an endothermic reaction, showing reactants, products, Ea and ΔH.

☐  I can explain why a reaction can give out a lot of energy and still be slow.

Cheat sheet

A collision leads to a reaction only if:

  1. the particles collide;
  2. they collide with at least the activation energy, Ea;
  3. they collide in a suitable orientation (facing the right way).

A collision that meets both conditions is a successful collision. Most collisions are not.

Definition

Activation energy (Ea): the minimum energy that colliding particles must have for the collision to result in a reaction. Unit: kJ mol−1.

Energy profiles. Ea is the height of the energy hill measured from the reactants. ΔH is the difference in level between reactants and products.

Signs of ΔH. Products lower than reactants: energy is given out, the reaction is exothermic and ΔH is negative. Products higher than reactants: energy is taken in, the reaction is endothermic and ΔH is positive.

Two different questions.

QuestionWhat answers it
How fast does the reaction go?The activation energy, and how often particles collide.
How much energy is given out or taken in?ΔH.

Hydrogen and oxygen can be mixed at room temperature and nothing visible happens until a spark is applied or a catalyst is added, although the reaction gives out a great deal of energy (ΔH is negative). The activation energy is so high that almost no collisions are successful until the spark gives some particles enough energy.

Common errors

  • “Every collision causes a reaction.” Most do not.
  • “Activation energy is the energy given out.” That is ΔH. Ea is the energy needed to get started.
  • “A big ΔH means a fast reaction.” ΔH tells us nothing about the rate.

3  What does concentration change?

Safety

  • Sodium thiosulfate solution: low hazard.
  • Dilute hydrochloric acid: irritant. Wear eye protection.
  • Sulfur dioxide gas is released and can trigger asthma. Use only the small volumes your teacher gives you, keep the room ventilated, and pour away the finished mixtures as your teacher directs.

Learning objectives

☐  I can name what we change, what we measure and what we keep the same when we time the disappearance of a cross.

☐  I can explain why the stand-in for rate can be used in place of the rate.

☐  I can describe the pattern between concentration and rate, using numbers from the data.

☐  I can explain the pattern with collisions: more particles in the same volume mean a higher collision frequency.

☐  I can sketch two Maxwell–Boltzmann curves for different concentrations, keeping Ea in the same place.

Cheat sheet

Sodium thiosulfate solution reacts with hydrochloric acid. Solid sulfur forms and makes the mixture cloudy. We time how long it takes for a cross under the flask to disappear.

Na2S2O3(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + S(s) + SO2(g) + H2O(l)
We…In this practical
changethe concentration of sodium thiosulfate solution
measurethe time, t, for the cross to disappear
keep the same (controlled variables)the temperature; the total volume of the mixture; the volume and concentration of acid; the flask and the cross; the person judging

Making the concentrations. Keep the total volume the same in every run. Then the volume of sodium thiosulfate solution tells us its concentration.

Worked example

20 cm3 of 0.10 mol dm−3 sodium thiosulfate solution, 20 cm3 of water and 10 cm3 of acid are mixed. Find the concentration of thiosulfate in the mixture.

Step 1. Find the total volume.

total volume = 20 + 20 + 10 = 50 cm3

Step 2. Write the formula, then the numbers, then calculate.

c(thiosulfate) = 
c(stock) × V(stock)
V(total)
c(thiosulfate) = 
0.10 × 20
50

The stand-in for rate. Each run needs the same amount of sulfur to hide the cross, so Δc is the same every time. Because rate is Δc over Δt and Δc does not change, a shorter time means a faster rate. We use this fraction as the stand-in for rate:

stand-in for rate = 
1
t

Its unit is s−1. We plot it against concentration. A straight line through the origin shows that the rate is proportional to the concentration: doubling the concentration doubles the rate.

As chemists, be on your guard!

The stand-in for rate is not the rate itself. The cross disappears when about the same amount of sulfur has formed, and judging “disappeared” depends on the observer. The same flask, the same cross and the same person keep the error small.

Left: the expected pattern. Right: more particles in the same volume lift the whole curve; the shape and the position of Ea stay the same. Darker grey: particles with at least Ea at the lower concentration; lighter grey: the extra particles at the higher concentration.

Explaining the pattern. A higher concentration puts more particles into the same volume. They collide more often (a higher collision frequency), so more successful collisions happen each second. The temperature has not changed, so the proportion of particles with at least Ea has not changed.

Writing frame: claim, evidence, reasoning

Claim. When the concentration of sodium thiosulfate increases, the rate             .

Evidence. At         mol dm−3 the time was        s; at         mol dm−3 the time was        s.

Reasoning. There are         particles in the same volume, so collisions are         frequent and         successful collisions happen each second. The temperature is the same, so the proportion of particles with at least Ea          .

Common errors

  • “A higher concentration gives the particles more energy.” It gives more collisions. The energy of each particle is unchanged.
  • “More particles” is not enough. Say more particles in the same volume.
  • Letting the total volume change between runs, so that the volume of thiosulfate no longer tells us the concentration.

4  What does temperature change?

Safety

  • Use the same reaction and hazards as stage 3.
  • Hot water: warm the solutions in a water bath, never over a flame, and stay below the temperature your teacher sets. More sulfur dioxide escapes from a warm mixture.

Learning objectives

☐  I can describe how temperature changes the time taken and the rate, using numbers from the data.

☐  I can state the two effects of raising the temperature, and say which matters more.

☐  I can sketch and label two Maxwell–Boltzmann curves for different temperatures, and shade the particles with at least Ea.

☐  I can explain why a rise of 10 °C roughly doubles the rate although collisions become only slightly more frequent.

☐  I can name the controlled variables in a temperature investigation.

Cheat sheet

Use the same reaction at several temperatures. Keep the concentrations and volumes the same. Warm the solutions in a water bath before mixing, and record the temperature of the mixture. As the temperature rises, t decreases and the stand-in for rate increases.

Two effects of raising the temperature.

EffectWhat changesHow big?
The particles move fasterCollisions are more frequentSmall: under 2% more collisions for a 10 °C rise near room temperature
More particles have at least EaA larger proportion of collisions are successfulLarge: this is the main reason the rate rises

Rule of thumb. For many reactions near room temperature, a rise of 10 °C roughly doubles the rate. It is a rule of thumb, not a law.

Darker grey: particles with at least Ea at the lower temperature. Lighter grey: the extra particles with at least Ea at the higher temperature.

Reading the curves.

  • Each curve shows how the particles in one sample are spread across different energies at one temperature.
  • The area under each curve is the total number of particles, so it is the same for both temperatures.
  • At the higher temperature the peak is lower and further to the right: the energies are spread over a wider range.
  • Neither curve comes back to touch the horizontal axis: a few particles always have a great deal of energy.
  • Ea is in the same place for both temperatures. Only the number of particles beyond it changes.
  • The shaded area beyond Ea shows the particles that can react when they collide. It is much larger at the higher temperature.

Writing frame: claim, evidence, reasoning

Claim. When the temperature increases, the rate             .

Evidence. At       °C the time was        s; at       °C the time was        s.

Reasoning. At the higher temperature the particles move        , so collisions are         frequent (a small effect). More importantly, a         proportion of particles have at least the activation energy, so a         proportion of collisions are successful.

Common errors

  • “Temperature only means more collisions.” The larger effect is that more collisions have enough energy.
  • Drawing the higher-temperature curve with a higher peak, or letting a curve touch the horizontal axis.
  • Moving Ea when the temperature changes. Ea stays where it is.

5  What does a catalyst change?

Safety

  • Hydrogen peroxide solution: irritant. Use the concentration your teacher gives you and wear eye protection.
  • Manganese(IV) oxide powder: avoid breathing the dust. Wash your hands afterwards.
  • Do not stopper the flask: oxygen is released and pressure must not build up.

Learning objectives

☐  I can define a catalyst.

☐  I can describe how to follow the catalysed decomposition of hydrogen peroxide by measuring the volume of oxygen against time.

☐  I can explain how a catalyst speeds up a reaction: an alternative pathway, a lower Ea, and it is not used up.

☐  I can draw an energy profile with and without a catalyst, with ΔH unchanged.

☐  I can show the effect of a catalyst on a Maxwell–Boltzmann diagram: the Ea marker moves left and the curve stays the same.

☐  I can read a volume–time graph and explain why the final volume is the same with and without a catalyst.

Cheat sheet

Definition

Catalyst: a substance that speeds up a reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy. It is not used up.

Hydrogen peroxide breaks down very slowly on its own. A little manganese(IV) oxide, MnO2, makes oxygen bubble off quickly. Yeast, which contains the enzyme catalase, does the same job. We collect the oxygen in a gas syringe and read the volume at regular times.

2H2O2(aq) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g)

A catalyst lowers Ea. On the profile the hump is lower; on the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve the Ea marker moves left, so more particles have enough energy. The curve itself does not change.

the activation energy (lower)
the rate (faster)		ΔH

the total amount of product made
the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve (only the Ea marker moves)

Volume of oxygen against time. The catalysed reaction is faster, but the same amount of hydrogen peroxide gives the same final volume.

As in stage 1, the slope of the graph is the rate. The catalysed curve is steeper at the start. It levels off sooner because the hydrogen peroxide is used up sooner.

Common errors

  • “The catalyst is used up.” It is not: it can be recovered unchanged at the end.
  • “A catalyst gives the particles more energy.” It lowers the energy they need.
  • “A catalyst changes ΔH or makes more product.” It changes neither.

Claim. Raising the temperature by 10 °C roughly doubled the rate.

Evidence. The time fell from 80 s to 38 s, so the stand-in for rate rose from 0.0125 s−1 to 0.0263 s−1, about 2.1 times bigger.

Reasoning. At 30 °C the particles move faster, so collisions are slightly more frequent. Mainly, a larger proportion of particles have at least the activation energy, so a larger proportion of collisions are successful.

Underlying ideas: definitions to learn

TermDefinition
activation energyThe minimum energy that colliding particles must have for the collision to result in a reaction. Symbol Ea. Unit: kJ mol−1.
catalystA substance that speeds up a reaction by giving it an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy. It is not used up.
collision frequencyHow many collisions happen per second in a given volume.
concentrationThe amount of substance in a given volume of solution, in mol dm−3. It tells us how crowded the particles are.
controlled variableA quantity we keep the same in every run, so that only the quantity we change can affect the result.
endothermicA reaction that takes in energy. The products are higher in energy than the reactants and the enthalpy change, ΔH, is positive.
energy profileA diagram of the energy of the particles as a reaction progresses, from reactants over an energy hill to products.
exothermicA reaction that gives out energy. The products are lower in energy than the reactants and the enthalpy change, ΔH, is negative.
Maxwell–Boltzmann distributionA graph showing how the particles in a sample are spread across different energies at one temperature. Most have middling energy; a few have a great deal.
proportionalTwo quantities are proportional when doubling one doubles the other.
rate of reactionThe change in concentration of a reactant or product divided by the time taken for that change. Unit: mol dm−3 s−1.
stand-in for rateA quantity that goes up and down in step with the rate when the same change happens in every run. In the disappearing cross we use 1 over t.
successful collisionA collision between particles with at least the activation energy and a suitable orientation, so that a reaction happens.

Scale-up question

A factory makes a product by reacting two gases, A and B, in a reaction vessel. The reaction is too slow. List three changes the engineers could make to speed it up. For each change, say what changes for the particles: the collision frequency, the proportion of particles with at least Ea, or Ea itself. (Hint: increasing the pressure of a gas squeezes more particles into the same volume.)
