What a formula tells you

A chemical formula is shorthand for a compound. FeSO₄ tells you which elements are present (iron, sulfur, oxygen), how many atoms of each (H₂SO₄ has 2 hydrogens, 1 sulfur, 4 oxygens), and — for compounds built from a named group — how the atoms are arranged: C₂H₅OH shows an ethyl group (C₂H₅) attached to a hydroxyl group (OH).

You don't need to memorise formulae. You need to know the charges on a handful of ions, and you can work the rest out.

Metal ions: read the charge from the group

  • Group 1 metals are always +1
  • Group 2 metals are always +2
  • Group 3 metals are always +3
  • Group 7 ions are always −1, group 6 ions −2, group 5 ions −3

Some metals — copper, iron, lead, tin, manganese, mercury — form more than one ion. The name tells you which: iron(II) chloride is Fe²⁺, so FeCl₂; iron(III) chloride is Fe³⁺, so FeCl₃.

A handful of ions don't come from the periodic table and just have to be learned: hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, ammonium NH₄⁺ — plus the fuller set in the reference tables below.

Balancing the charges

Whatever ions you're combining, the positive and negative charges must add to zero. Sodium sulfate: Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ — you need two sodiums to cancel the two-minus on sulfate, giving Na₂SO₄. Calcium hydrogen carbonate: Ca²⁺ and HCO₃⁻ — you need two hydrogencarbonates to cancel the two-plus on calcium, giving Ca(HCO₃)₂.

Brackets. Put brackets around a group only when it's multiplied by 2 or more and made of more than one element. MgBr₂ needs no bracket (bromine is one element). Ca(OH)₂ needs one — CaOH₂ would be wrong. NaOH needs no bracket either, because there's only one hydroxide.
Cancelling. You can usually simplify a ratio — Ca₂(CO₃)₂ becomes CaCO₃ — but never do this for organic compounds: C₂H₄ stays C₂H₄, not CH₂.

Non-metals: count the bonds instead

Compounds of two non-metals are covalent, not ionic — there's no charge to balance, but each element has a typical number of bonds it forms. Balance those instead: sulfur dioxide is SO₂ because sulfur's two bonds are satisfied by two oxygens, each using one of its two bonds.
