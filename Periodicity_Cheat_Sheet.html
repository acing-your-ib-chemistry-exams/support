3.1

The Periodic Table

Arrangement by atomic number reveals the periodicity of electron configuration.

Position = configuration. The block tells you which sub-level holds the highest-energy electron; the period number is the outer (highest) occupied energy level, n; the group number gives the number of valence electrons (main groups).

3.2a

Effective Nuclear Charge

The engine behind every trend below.

Outer electrons are attracted by the full nuclear charge Z but shielded (repelled) by the inner, core electrons. The charge they actually experience is the effective nuclear charge, Zeff ≈ Z − (number of shielding/core electrons).

ElementNaMgAlSi
Nuclear charge11121314
Core (shielding) e⁻10101010
Zeff on valence e⁻≈ +1≈ +2≈ +3≈ +4
Across a period: one proton added, no new shielding shell ⇒ Zeff rises with group number. Down a group: one full shell of core electrons is added along with the protons ⇒ Zeff stays roughly constant (≈ +1 for Group 1, ≈ +7 for Group 17, etc.).
3.2b

The Five Trends

All explained by the balance of Zeff against the number of occupied shells.

PropertyAcross a period (L→R)Down a groupWhy
Atomic radius ↓ decreases ↑ increases Across: Zeff rises, same shell pulled in tighter. Down: an extra occupied shell each row outweighs the near-constant Zeff.
Ionic radius cations shrink Gp 1→14; anions shrink Gp 14→17 (big jump at the metal/non-metal switch) ↑ increases Cations are smaller than their parent atom (outer shell lost); anions are larger (extra e⁻–e⁻ repulsion). Isoelectronic species shrink as Z increases.
Ionization energy ↑ increases (dips after Gp 2→13 and Gp 15→16) ↓ decreases Across: stronger pull on outer e⁻. Down: electron removed from a shell further away; shielding keeps Zeff ~constant.
Electron affinity generally ↑ more exothermic to Gp 17 (Gp 18 ≈ 0; Gp 2 & 15 anomalously low) ↓ less exothermic Same logic as IE, displaced one group and inverted in sign — high Zeff (Gp 17, ≈+7) attracts the incoming electron most strongly.
Electronegativity ↑ increases ↓ decreases Measures pull on bonding electrons in a covalent bond — same Zeff/shielding argument as ionization energy. F = 4.0 (highest); Cs = 0.7 (lowest). Not defined for Group 18.
Ionization energy discontinuities (Period 2/3, general pattern): Group 13 (ns²np¹) is lower than Group 2 (ns²) — the single p electron is higher in energy than a full s sub-level. Group 16 (ns²np⁴) is lower than Group 15 (ns²np³) — the fourth p electron is paired and repelled by its partner, so easier to remove.
Electron affinity exceptions: Be (Gp 2) is endothermic — the incoming electron is repelled by the filled 2s sub-level. N (Gp 15) is only weakly exothermic — the electron must pair up in an already half-filled 2p sub-level (extra repulsion).

The Trends, Plotted

The same six patterns, as data — this is what the zigzags and dips actually look like.

Figure 3.4
First ionization energy — H to Ca
Peaks at every noble gas, troughs at every Group 1 metal; small dips after Gp 2 (→13) and Gp 15 (→16) each period.
Figure 3.5
Electron affinity — first 18 elements
Most exothermic (most negative) at the halogens; anomalous spikes at Be and N break the smooth trend.
Figure 3.11
Atomic radius — Period 4 (K to Br)
Falls fast from K to Sc, then barely moves across the d block — the added d electrons shield the 4s/4p electrons almost as well as they're attracted.
Figure 3.10
Electronegativity — Period 3 (Na to Cl)
A smooth, almost linear rise — no sub-shell discontinuities here, because electronegativity reflects the whole bonding environment, not one electron's removal.
Figure 3.7
Melting point — Periods 2 & 3
Rises to a giant-covalent peak at Group 14 (C, Si), then collapses through simple-molecular Groups 15–18 — bonding type, not just charge, is driving this one.
Figure 3.8
Density vs relative atomic mass (Lothar Meyer, 1869)
Not one of the five trends above, but the historical evidence for periodicity itself — Meyer plotted this independently of Mendeleev, the same year. Elements in the same group cluster near the same point on the curve.
3.2c

Group 1 vs Group 17

Opposite ends of the table, opposite trends in reactivity.

Group 1 — Alkali metals

Li · Na · K · Rb · Cs · Fr — form M⁺
  • Soft, shiny when cut, low density, good conductors
  • Reactivity increases down the group — lower ionization energy makes the outer electron easier to lose
  • React with water to give H2(g) + metal hydroxide (alkaline solution)
  • Melting point decreases down the group (weaker metallic bonding as ionic radius grows)
Reaction with water
2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g)
Reaction with a halogen
2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)

Group 17 — Halogens

F · Cl · Br · I · At — form X⁻ (diatomic X₂)
  • Coloured; state runs gas (F₂, Cl₂) → liquid (Br₂) → solid (I₂)
  • Reactivity decreases down the group — larger atomic radius weakens the pull on an incoming electron
  • Form ionic halides with metals, covalent compounds with other non-metals
  • Melting point increases down the group (stronger London/dispersion forces, more electrons)
Displacement (more reactive halogen wins)
Cl₂(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br₂(aq)
Silver halide precipitate test
Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s)
The most vigorous alkali/halogen reaction pairs the elements furthest apart in the table: francium (lowest IE in Gp 1) with fluorine (highest Zeff, most exothermic EA, in Gp 17). AgCl = white, AgBr = cream, AgI = pale yellow — a classic halide test.

Metallic Behaviour & Period 3 Oxides

Bonding — and therefore acid–base character — shifts from ionic to covalent across the period.

Oxidation no.+1+2+3+4+5 / +6+7

Ionic character falls across the period as each element's electronegativity climbs toward oxygen's (3.4) — the electronegativity difference shrinks, so bonding becomes more covalent and oxides become more acidic.

Al₂O₃(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂O(l)
Al₂O₃(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 3H₂O(l) → 2NaAl(OH)₄(aq)

Definitions You Must Know

Every term this unit rests on, pulled from the class reagent-label deck.

The table itself

Periodic tableA table of all the known chemical elements, arranged in order of atomic number into rows (periods) and columns (groups) of elements with similar properties.
PeriodA horizontal row of elements in the Periodic Table.
GroupA vertical column in the Periodic Table.
Periodic lawThe physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.
PeriodicityA repeating pattern of atomic, physical and chemical properties across a period in the Periodic Table.
ElementA chemical which cannot be broken down into simpler substances; contains only one type of atom.

Families

Alkali metalsThe elements of Group 1 (lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium and caesium).
Alkaline earth metalsThe elements of Group 2 (beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium and radium).
HalogensThe elements of Group 17 — reactive non-metals that exist as diatomic molecules, e.g. fluorine, chlorine, bromine.
Noble gasesThe elements of Group 18 — very unreactive gases with a full outer shell of electrons, e.g. helium, neon, argon.
ChalcogensThe elements of Group 16, including oxygen and sulfur.
Transition elementA d-block element which forms one or more stable ions with an incompletely filled set of d-orbitals.

Atomic structure

AtomThe smallest particle of an element which can take part in a chemical reaction.
Atomic number (Z)The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom — also called proton number.
Mass number (A)The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
IonAn electrically charged particle formed from an atom or group of atoms by loss or gain of electrons, e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻.
Relative atomic massThe ratio of the average mass per atom of an element relative to 1/12 the mass of an atom of ¹²C.

Trend quantities

First ionisation energyThe energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state, to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
First electron affinityThe enthalpy change when one mole of electrons is added to one mole of gaseous atoms, to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions.
ElectronegativityThe ability of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
Ionic bondThe electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
Metallic bondThe electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.
Covalent bondThe strong electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the bonded atoms.
