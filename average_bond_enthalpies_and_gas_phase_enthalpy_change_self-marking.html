Learning objectives

  • describe what an average bond enthalpy is, and why bond enthalpies are used for the gas phase;

Making and breaking bonds

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. In the gas phase, H–H(g) → H(g) + H(g). Energy has to be put in to pull the atoms apart.

  1. Breaking a bond takes in energy: endothermic.
  2. Forming a bond releases energy: exothermic.
  3. H–H has an average bond enthalpy of 436 kJ mol−1: 436 kJ is needed to break one mole of H–H bonds, and 436 kJ is released when one mole forms.
Method: what to do
  • Breaking bonds takes in energy: endothermic.
  • Forming bonds releases energy: exothermic.
  • The average bond enthalpy is the energy needed to break one mole of a bond in the gas phase, in kJ mol−1. The same energy is released when one mole of the bond forms.
  • A bigger number means a stronger bond.
  • Bond enthalpies are used for gases only, (g).

Counting the bonds in a molecule

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. Count the bonds in one molecule of H2O (H–O–H).

  1. Two lines join atoms: both are O–H bonds.
  2. H2O has 2 O–H bonds.
  3. Total bonds in one molecule: 2.
Method: what to do
  1. Look at the structural formula. Every line (or pair or trio of lines) joining two atoms is one bond.
  2. A double bond, O=O or C=O, counts as one bond of that type. So does a triple bond, N≡N.
  3. Count each type of bond one at a time.

Energy to break all the bonds in one molecule

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. The energy to break all the bonds in 1 mol of H2O(g).

  1. H2O has 2 O–H bonds.
  2. Table 1: O–H = 464 kJ mol−1.
  3. 2 × 464 = 928 kJ.
Method: what to do
  1. Count the bonds of each type in one molecule.
  2. Look up each bond in Table 1: the bond is in the top row, its average bond enthalpy is directly beneath.
  3. Use this equation:
    energy = number of bonds × average bond enthalpy
  4. Do this for each type of bond and add the answers.

Example: H2O has 2 O–H bonds. 2 × 464 = 928 kJ.

Energy for one side of an equation

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. Bonds broken in the reactants of H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g).

  1. H2 has 1 H–H bond. Cl2 has 1 Cl–Cl bond.
  2. Table 1: H–H = 436 and Cl–Cl = 243.
  3. 436 + 243 = 679 kJ taken in.
Method: what to do
  1. Count all the bonds on one side of the equation.
  2. A big number in front of a formula means that many molecules: multiply the bonds in one molecule by that number.
  3. Number of bonds × average bond enthalpy (Table 1), added up.
  4. Reactants side: energy taken in (bonds broken). Products side: energy released (bonds formed).

Example: 2H2O has 2 × 2 = 4 O–H bonds.

Estimating the enthalpy change, ΔH

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g).

  1. Bonds broken: 1 H–H + 1 Cl–Cl = 436 + 243 = 679 kJ taken in.
  2. Bonds formed: 2 H–Cl = 2 × 432 = 864 kJ released.
  3. 864 is bigger than 679: more energy is released, so the reaction is exothermic.
  4. Size of ΔH = 864 − 679 = 185.
  5. Exothermic, so ΔH = −185 kJ mol−1.
Method: what to do
  1. Bonds broken (reactants): count, then number × bond enthalpy, then add: energy in.
  2. Bonds formed (products): count, then number × bond enthalpy, then add: energy out.
  3. Which total is bigger? Out bigger: exothermic, ΔH negative. In bigger: endothermic, ΔH positive.
  4. Use this equation for the size:
    size of ΔH = bigger total − smaller total
  5. Put the sign on last.

This is the same as ΔH = energy to break bonds − energy released forming bonds.

Larger molecules and endothermic reactions

Stretch: secure this to reach towards a 5.

Worked example

Example. 2NH3(g) → N2(g) + 3H2(g).

  1. Bonds broken: 2 × 3 = 6 N–H = 6 × 391 = 2346 kJ taken in.
  2. Bonds formed: 1 N≡N + 3 H–H = 945 + 1308 = 2253 kJ released.
  3. 2346 is bigger than 2253: more energy is taken in, so the reaction is endothermic.
  4. Size of ΔH = 2346 − 2253 = 93.
  5. Endothermic, so ΔH = +93 kJ mol−1.
Method: what to do
  1. Work out the energy in (bonds broken) and the energy out (bonds formed).
  2. Count all the bonds with care: multiply by the number in front of each formula.
  3. Out bigger: exothermic, ΔH negative. In bigger: endothermic, ΔH positive.
  4. size of ΔH = bigger total − smaller total
  5. The answers are estimates, because the bond enthalpies are averages.

Working out an unknown bond enthalpy

Stretch: secure this to reach towards a 5.

Worked example

Example. H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g), ΔH = −185 kJ mol−1. Find the H–Cl bond enthalpy.

  1. Energy taken in: 436 + 243 = 679 kJ.
  2. Exothermic, so the energy released is bigger: 679 + 185 = 864 kJ.
  3. Two H–Cl bonds form: 864 ÷ 2 = 432 kJ mol−1.
Method: what to do
  1. Work out the energy taken in for the reactants (all bond enthalpies known).
  2. Exothermic (ΔH negative): energy released is bigger.
    energy released = energy taken in + size of ΔH
  3. Divide by the number of unknown bonds formed:
    average bond enthalpy = energy released ÷ number of bonds

Underlying ideas: definitions to learn

Average bond enthalpy
The average energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond in gaseous molecules, in kJ mol−1. It is an average over many different compounds.
Covalent bond
A bond in which two atoms share electrons. A single bond shares one pair, a double bond two pairs and a triple bond three pairs.
Endothermic
A reaction that absorbs energy from the surroundings. More energy is taken in breaking bonds than is released forming bonds. ΔH is positive.
Enthalpy change, ΔH
The energy change of a reaction, in kJ mol−1. ΔH is negative for an exothermic reaction and positive for an endothermic reaction.
Exothermic
A reaction that releases energy to the surroundings. More energy is released forming bonds than is taken in breaking bonds. ΔH is negative.
Gas phase
The state symbol (g). In a gas the molecules are far apart, so the only energy changes come from breaking and making bonds inside molecules.
Molecule
A group of atoms joined by covalent bonds, such as H2O or CH4.
