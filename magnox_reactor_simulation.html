A magnox reactor: from control rods to electricity

A magnox reactor burns natural uranium metal. The uranium fuel rods are sealed in a thin skin of magnox alloy, and they sit in channels in a huge stack of graphite blocks. Carbon dioxide gas is blown up through the channels. It carries the heat to a boiler, and the steam from the boiler turns a turbine. The first station of this kind, Calder Hall in Cumbria, fed electricity into the grid in 1956.

Below, you control the reactor. Move the control rods and watch the rate of fission change. Change the speed of the gas blowers and watch the temperatures. Then watch the electricity appear in the turbine hall.

Try these

  1. Start-up. The rods begin fully inserted. Withdraw them slowly. Watch k. At what rod position does the rate of fission stop falling and start to rise? What is k at that moment?
  2. Full power. Bring the fission rate to about 100 %. Read the electricity in MWe. Where are the rods now? Compare this with your start-up position and explain the difference using the temperature of the core.
  3. Withdraw the rods further. Watch the fission rate and the cladding temperature as you pull the rods out of the core. What happens and why does it happen quickly?
  4. Less gas. At full power, halve the blower speed. Describe what happens to the cladding temperature, the gas temperature, the fission rate and the electricity. Which changes first?
  5. Shut down. Insert all rods, then turn the blowers to zero. Describe the fission rate, then the cladding temperature. Where does the heat come from?
  6. Challenging. Find a rod position and a blower speed that give a steady 25 MWe.

How the model works

The control rods are made of boron steel. Boron absorbs neutrons, so inserting a rod lowers k. Graphite slows the fast neutrons from fission so that uranium-235 can capture them. In the model the rate of fission changes like this:

change in fission ratechange in time = k − 1neutron lifetime × fission rate

When k is more than 1 the numerator is positive and the fission rate rises; when k is less than 1 it falls. Gas carries the heat away. The rise in gas temperature is:

rise in gas temperature = heat removed by the gasgas flow × heat capacity of the gas

Hotter fuel lowers k a little, so the reactor steadies itself. Switching off the chain reaction does not switch off the heat: the fission products keep releasing about 6 % of full power, falling slowly, as decay heat. The boiler turns water to steam, and the turbine and generator change a share of that heat into electricity: the hotter the gas leaving the core, the larger the share.

Underlying ideas: definitions to learn

TermMeaning
moderatorA material, here graphite, that slows fast neutrons so that they are more likely to cause fission.
control rodA rod of a neutron-absorbing material, here boron steel, that is moved in or out of the core to change k.
fuel rodNatural uranium metal in a magnox alloy skin. About 0.7 % of the uranium is uranium-235, which is the part that undergoes fission.
coolantThe carbon dioxide gas that carries heat out of the core.
neutron multiplication factor, kThe number of neutrons in one generation divided by the number in the one before. When k is 1 the chain reaction is steady.
SCRAMAn emergency shutdown, in which all the control rods are driven fully into the core.
decay heatHeat from the radioactive decay of fission products, released after the chain reaction stops.
heat exchanger (boiler)A set of tubes in which hot gas turns water to steam without the gas and water mixing.
