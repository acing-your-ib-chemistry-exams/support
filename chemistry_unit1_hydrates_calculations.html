Activity 4: Calculations Involving Hydrates of Metal Compounds

Many metal salts crystallise with water locked into their structure — this is the water of crystallisation. First, a reminder of how to work out the molar mass of a hydrate. Then the real skill: heating a hydrate to drive the water off, and using the masses before and after to work out exactly how many water molecules were there in the first place. Lots of examples. Self-marking, with hints, and extra practice folded in automatically if a question doesn't go your way.

Glossary

Water of crystallisation
Water molecules built into the crystal structure of certain salts, in a fixed ratio — shown in the formula after a dot, e.g. the "5H₂O" in CuSO₄·5H₂O.
Hydrate (hydrated salt)
A compound whose crystals include water of crystallisation.
Anhydrous
"Without water" — a compound with no water of crystallisation, either because it never had any, or because it has been driven off by heating.
Heating to constant mass
Heating, cooling and reweighing a sample repeatedly until two weighings in a row give the same mass. That steady final mass is proof that all the water has been driven off — and that you haven't stopped weighing too early.
Formula of a hydrate
Written as anhydrous formula · x H₂O, e.g. MgSO₄·7H₂O. The molar mass is the Mr of the anhydrous part, plus x lots of the molar mass of water (18).

A. Molar mass of hydrates reminder

A hydrate's formula has two parts: the anhydrous compound, and the water of crystallisation, joined by a dot. To find the molar mass, work out the Mr of the anhydrous part as normal, then add on the water — the molar mass of H₂O is 18, so multiply 18 by however many water molecules the formula shows, and add it on.

Worked example Find the molar mass of copper(II) sulfate crystals, CuSO₄·5H₂O.
  1. Mr of anhydrous CuSO₄ = 64 + 32 + (4 × 16) = 160
  2. Mass of water = 5 × 18 = 90
  3. Total Mr = 160 + 90 = 250
Molar mass of CuSO₄·5H₂O = 250.

B. Hydrate analysis: the method new idea

Now run the idea the other way. Heat a weighed sample of a hydrate strongly, and the water of crystallisation drives off as steam, leaving the anhydrous salt behind. To be sure all the water has gone — and that you haven't just stopped part-way — you heat, cool and reweigh the sample again and again, until the mass stops changing between two weighings in a row. That's heating to constant massRepeatedly heating, cooling and reweighing a sample until consecutive weighings agree — proof that no more mass is being lost..

From the mass lost, you can work out exactly how many water molecules were in the original formula — the value of x in anhydrous · xH₂O.

Four steps, every time:

  1. Mass of water lost = mass of hydrate − mass of anhydrous salt (after heating to constant mass).
  2. Moles of water = mass of water ÷ 18 (the molar mass of H₂O).
  3. Moles of anhydrous salt = mass of anhydrous salt ÷ its molar mass.
  4. x = moles of water ÷ moles of anhydrous salt.
Worked example A student heats 25.0 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, to constant mass. 16.0 g of anhydrous CuSO₄ (Mr = 160) remains.
  1. Mass of water lost = 25.0 − 16.0 = 9.0 g
  2. Moles of water = 9.0 ÷ 18 = 0.5 mol
  3. Moles of CuSO₄ = 16.0 ÷ 160 = 0.1 mol
  4. x = 0.5 ÷ 0.1 = 5
Formula = CuSO₄·5H₂O.

6. Why do we heat the sample to constant mass, rather than just heating it once and weighing it?

HintIf you stopped too early, some water would still be trapped in the crystals — and the mass would be wrong.
Model answer (advanced)Weighing only once risks under-heating, leaving some water behind and giving a final mass that's too high — which would make the calculated x too small.

7. A hydrate sample has a mass of 21.9 g before heating and 11.1 g after heating to constant mass. What mass of water was lost?

HintMass of water lost = mass of hydrate − mass of anhydrous salt.
Model answer (advanced)21.9 − 11.1 = 10.8 g. (This is the CaCl₂·6H₂O data from the sections below — you'll use it again shortly.)

E. Spot the mistake diagnostic

15. Working out x for a hydrate, a student wrote: "moles of anhydrous salt ÷ moles of water = x." What's wrong with this?

HintThere's always more moles of water than moles of the anhydrous salt (that's the whole point of x). Which order gives a number bigger than 1?
Model answer (advanced)Flipping the division gives a fraction less than 1 instead of a whole number like 5, 6, 7 or 10 — an easy way to notice the mistake even without redoing the working.

16. A student has 21.9 g of hydrate and 11.1 g of anhydrous salt left after heating. They calculate x as 21.9 ÷ 11.1 = 1.97, and round it to 2. What's the actual mistake here?

HintWater and the anhydrous salt have very different molar masses — a ratio of grams is not the same as a ratio of moles.
Model answer (advanced)This is CaCl₂·6H₂O data from question 12 — the real answer is x = 6, not 2. Grams of hydrate compared directly to grams of anhydrous salt ignores that water (Mr = 18) and CaCl₂ (Mr = 111) don't weigh the same per mole, so the ratio of masses is nothing like the ratio of moles.

F. Terminology check

17. Which is the correct definition of "anhydrous"?

HintThe prefix "an-" means "without", like "anaerobic" means without air.
Model answer (advanced)Anhydrous means without water — the state a hydrate reaches once it's been heated to constant mass.

18. In the formula MgSO₄·7H₂O, the dot separates the water of crystallisation from the ______ part of the compound.

HintThis is the same word as in question 17.
Model answer (advanced)Anhydrous — the MgSO₄ part, which is what remains once every water molecule has been driven off.

19. Which best explains why a student re-weighs a heated hydrate sample more than once before trusting the final mass?

HintLook back at the definition of "heating to constant mass" in the glossary.
Model answer (advanced)If the mass were still dropping, there'd still be water left to drive off — a single weighing can't tell you that on its own.

Bonus round going further

20. A student heats 4.92 g of magnesium sulfate crystals, MgSO₄·7H₂O (Mr = 246), to constant mass. What mass of anhydrous MgSO₄ (Mr = 120) should remain?

HintThis is the reverse direction — start by finding moles of the whole hydrate: 4.92 ÷ 246.
Model answer (advanced)Moles of hydrate = 4.92 ÷ 246 = 0.02 mol. Since each mole of hydrate contains one mole of MgSO₄, mass of MgSO₄ = 0.02 × 120 = 2.4 g.

21. What percentage of the mass of CuSO₄·5H₂O (Mr = 250) is water? Give your answer as a whole number, without the % sign.

HintMass of water per mole = 5 × 18 = 90. Percentage = (90 ÷ 250) × 100.
Model answer (advanced)(90 ÷ 250) × 100 = 36%. This is why heating a hydrate can lose well over a third of its mass — worth remembering next time you see a "before and after" hydrate question.
