Part 7 One Company Dissolves, Another Cannot Afford the Gas: 1990–Present The last reading in this series: what reunification's economics actually did to Leuna and Buna, how three national chemical giants became something bigger and stranger, and why the company this whole series started with is, in 2026, struggling to afford its own gas bill

Part 6 ended on 3 October 1990, deliberately without following Leuna, Buna or the rest of the former East German chemical industry into what reunification actually did to them. This reading picks that up, and carries the whole series — Ludwigshafen, Leverkusen, Frankfurt, Leuna, the Buna works, and Motorenfabrik Oberursel — all the way to the present day. It closes a story that began, in Part 1, with a scattering of small chemical trades along the Rhine and Main before 1800.

Part 7 · draft 1990 – present Ludwigshafen · Leverkusen · Frankfurt · Leuna · Schkopau · Oberursel

This is the last reading in the series, and it is built around a fact that would have sounded absurd to almost anyone in either Germany in 1990: within a few years of reunification, two of the most fought-over industrial sites in this entire story — Leuna and Buna, built by IG Farben, nationalised by the Soviets, run for decades as flagships of a planned economy — ended up owned by a French oil major and an American chemical company. Meanwhile, in the West, the three companies this series has followed since 1952 stopped being simply German companies at all: one dissolved into a French-German pharmaceutical group and no longer exists under its own name; another became, through its biggest acquisition ever, the centre of one of the largest product-liability disputes in corporate history; and the third — BASF, the very company this whole series began with in Part 2 — is, as this is being written, struggling with an energy bill it cannot make work at its own founding site.

Three threads run through it, in roughly chronological order. First, what privatisation actually did to Leuna and Buna in the 1990s — not as an abstract economic story, but in terms of jobs, ownership, and what physically happened to the plants. Second, the West German successors' continued consolidation into global companies that, by the 2000s and 2010s, were barely recognisable as the tidy three-way 1952 split Part 5 described. Third, the present-day energy crisis at Ludwigshafen, which closes the series roughly where it can be closed responsibly: not with a tidy ending, but with an open, ongoing problem.

Part one · 1990–2004

What reunification actually did to Leuna and Buna

After 1989, the former East German state's industrial holdings, including Leuna and Buna, passed to the TreuhandanstaltTreuhandanstalt — the West German state agency set up in 1990 to privatise, restructure or liquidate the roughly 8,000 formerly state-owned enterprises of East Germany., the West German agency created specifically to privatise, restructure, or shut down the several thousand state-owned enterprises the GDR had left behind. Neither site survived this process intact, and neither was simply sold whole to a single buyer — both were broken apart.

Leuna's oil refinery went, in the early 1990s, to the French energy company Elf Aquitaine (later absorbed into Total), which built an entirely new, modern refinery on the site — the MIDER plant, later renamed TRM — that began production in 1997: a genuinely large piece of French investment landing in what had been, six years earlier, Soviet-administered territory. The rest of the old Leuna works was broken into pieces sold separately to different buyers, including BASF itself, the industrial-gas firm Linde, and the Belgian plastics group DOMO, with a new joint infrastructure company, InfraLeuna, created just to keep water, power, and site services running across all the resulting fragments. The workforce fell from around 28,000 in the late 1970s to roughly 9,000 by 2014, as the older, less competitive parts of the old plant were closed rather than modernised.

Buna followed a similar, if somewhat harder, path. Employment collapsed from around 18,000 under GDR management to just 7,200 by 1992, before the American company Dow Chemical took over large parts of the site in 1995 — acquiring, in the historian's phrasing available to us, “large parts of the facilities, but only a few of the employees.” Dow rebuilt much of the plant around modern petroleum-based production; since 2004 the site has operated as Dow Olefinverbund GmbH, a Dow subsidiary. Decontaminating and rehabilitating the surrounding land — the accumulated pollution Part 6 described — cost the privatisation agency an estimated 809 million Deutschmarks in 1999 alone. In 2021, part of the site's synthetic-rubber production was sold on again, this time to the Polish chemical company Synthos: a reminder that this process of buying, closing and reselling pieces of the old IG Farben sites has not, even now, fully stopped.

As historians, be on your guard! — two numbers that both matter

It is tempting to reduce this to a single figure — jobs lost — and treat it as the whole story. But two things are true at once: the scale of job losses at both sites was genuinely severe and fell on real communities with no real say in the matter, and the environmental rehabilitation these sites needed (that 809-million-Deutschmark figure is for one year, at one site) was itself a direct, delayed cost of the pollution Part 6 described, not a cost the privatisation created out of nothing. Assigning blame cleanly between “the plan that polluted” and “the market that closed things down” is harder than either side of that argument usually admits.

Part two · 1999–2018

The tidy three-way split stops being tidy

Part 5's 1952 breakup of IG Farben into BASF, Bayer and Hoechst held, as a clean three-way structure, for almost half a century. It did not last through this reading's period. In 1999, Hoechst merged with the French company Rhône-Poulenc to form a new pharmaceutical group, Aventis, based not in Frankfurt but in Strasbourg — meaning the Hoechst name, and the independent company Part 6 described building the Trevira fibre business, stopped existing as a going concern. Aventis itself lasted only a few years before its own 2004 merger with Sanofi-Synthélabo produced Sanofi-Aventis, and by 2005 the remaining Hoechst legal entity had become simply a wholly owned subsidiary of what is today called Sanofi. Of the three great dye firms Part 2 introduced founding themselves in the 1860s, one has now, in every meaningful sense, ceased to exist under its own name.

Bayer took a different, more dramatic path: growth by acquisition on a scale even BASF's 1969 Wintershall purchase did not approach. On 7 June 2018, Bayer completed its purchase of the American agricultural giant MonsantoMonsanto — an American agrochemical and agricultural biotechnology company, acquired by Bayer in 2018 for $63 billion in Bayer's largest-ever acquisition, bringing with it extensive product-liability litigation over its glyphosate weedkiller. for $63 billion including debt — the largest acquisition in Bayer's history, combining Bayer's crop-protection chemistry with Monsanto's seed and genetic-engineering business. The deal came with a cost few chemical mergers in this series have carried: Monsanto's glyphosate-based weedkiller, Roundup, was already the subject of litigation in the United States over claims it caused cancer, and Bayer inherited tens of thousands of lawsuits along with the company, a legal and financial burden that has weighed on Bayer's share price and reputation ever since.

What stayed recognisably the same

BASF is the one member of the original 1952 trio still trading today under its own name, on largely its own historic site at Ludwigshafen, as an independent German chemical company — which is precisely why it, rather than Bayer or the former Hoechst, carries this reading's closing section.

Part three · 2022–present

The company this series began with cannot afford its own gas bill

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, and the collapse of Russian pipeline gas supply to Europe that followed, hit no major European industrial site harder than BASF's home works at Ludwigshafen — the same site Part 2 followed from its 1865 founding. BASF's own figures put the extra cost of natural gas across its European operations in 2022 alone at $2.9 billion, on top of sales volumes at the company that fell 7% that year, against a 12% fall in German chemical-industry output as a whole. In response, BASF announced in early 2023 that it would permanently close one of Ludwigshafen's two ammonia plants — the single largest natural-gas consumer on the entire site, and, in a straight line back to Part 3, a direct descendant of the Haber–Bosch chemistry this series has followed since the 1910s — along with associated nitrogen-fertiliser and specialty-chemical plants making caprolactam, adipic acid and polyurethane precursors. Around 2,600 jobs, roughly 2.3% of BASF's global workforce, were cut across 2023–24, with some 700 of those at Ludwigshafen itself.

As historians, be on your guard! — this is not yet history

Everything in this section describes events from the last few years, some from the last few months before this reading was written. It is drawn from contemporary business reporting rather than the settled historical accounts the rest of this series has been able to draw on, and it may look different in ten years' time than it does now — BASF might recover fully, might restructure further, or something in between. This reading includes it anyway, because ending a seven-part series on the safely distant past would quietly suggest history stops being made once it becomes uncomfortable to assess. It does not.

BASF frames these closures as permanent, not temporary: the company has said the affected ammonia and fertiliser capacity will not return even if gas prices eventually fall, and is instead investing in production capacity elsewhere, including in China. Whether Ludwigshafen a decade from now still resembles the site this series has followed since 1865, or has become substantially smaller as BASF's global centre of gravity shifts elsewhere, is a genuinely open question — one this reading cannot answer, because it has not happened yet.

Meanwhile, in the Taunus hills · 1990–present

The smallest site in this series, still open for business

Of every site this series has followed, only one is, as far as this reading can establish, doing much the same work today that it was doing when this reading's period began. Motorenfabrik Oberursel — sold to what was then BMW Rolls-Royce in the same year as reunification, as Part 6 described — continues to operate today as part of Rolls-Royce Deutschland, developing and building jet engines in the Rolls-Royce BR700 family and its successors, in the same Taunus market town this series first visited in Part 3. No merger has dissolved it into a larger foreign entity; no energy crisis has forced its closure; no privatisation agency has had to break it into pieces. It is, admittedly, a much smaller and much less politically weighted site than Leuna, Buna or Ludwigshafen ever were — but its continuity, of all things, is the one genuinely stable thread this entire seven-part series has to offer.

The close · 1800–present

Where this series actually ends

This series began, in Part 1, with a scattering of small chemical trades along the Rhine and Main before 1800, and the question of how industrialised Germany really was at that point (not very, it turned out). It followed those trades through the founding of Bayer, BASF, Hoechst and Cassella in the 1860s; through the Haber–Bosch process and a war that a British naval blockade helped decide; through a merger that created, briefly, the largest chemical company on Earth; through that company's capture by a genocidal regime and its use of slave labour beside a death camp; through a legal reckoning that mostly wasn't one; through four decades of the same industry rebuilt as two rival economic systems either side of one border; and now, here, to a set of companies that are French-German pharmaceutical groups, defendants in mass litigation, or simply trying to keep the lights on at a plant founded in 1865.

This reading, and this series, ends without a tidy conclusion, because the industry it describes has not reached one. That is not a failure of the writing. It is what an honest history of something still happening actually looks like.

Interlude

Finishing the table: the last nine elements

This series has followed the periodic table since 1800, from twenty-five known elements to all ninety-two natural ones, complete by 1951. The last nine elements on it — darmstadtium, roentgenium and copernicium from that same German laboratory in Darmstadt, and six more from Russian, American and Japanese collaborations — were made between 1994 and 2010, completing the table exactly as it stands today, 118 elements wide, with not one atom of the last nine ever existing anywhere outside the accelerator that made it. The 109 elements known by 1990

This baseline is exactly where The Periodic Table in 1951: Beyond Uranium leaves off: ninety-eight elements known by 1951, natural and laboratory-made together, and eleven more added over the following four decades in the contested, sometimes genuinely disputed race between American, Soviet and, from 1981, German laboratories. The last three of those eleven — bohrium, hassium and meitnerium — came from the GSI laboratory in Darmstadt, West Germany, using the precise one-atom-at-a-time technique that would go on to finish the table almost entirely.

Click through to today — finishing the table

GSI's Darmstadt laboratory went on, through the 1990s, to make three more elements with the very same cold-fusion technique that had produced bohrium, hassium and meitnerium: darmstadtium (1994), roentgenium (1994) and copernicium (1996), named respectively for the city itself, for the discoverer of X-rays, and for Copernicus. Germany's run of new elements, which had begun at the very end of Part 6's story, ended there, but the wider hunt for the last empty squares on the table did not. Element 113, nihonium, was made in 2004 at the RIKEN laboratory in Japan — the first element ever discovered in Asia, and named, fittingly, for Japan itself (Nihon). The remaining five — flerovium (1998), livermorium (2000), moscovium (2003), tennessine (2010) and oganesson (2002) — came from a sustained collaboration between the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research at Dubna, Russia, and the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in the United States, with tennessine also crediting Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Vanderbilt University in Tennessee. Oganesson, element 118, is named for the Russian physicist Yuri Oganessian, who led much of this later work himself — one of only two elements on the entire table named for someone still living at the time. With its discovery in 2002, the periodic table reached its seventh full period and, for now, its end: no laboratory anywhere has yet confirmed a new element since.

Looking back on the whole series

Questions to think about

  1. Leuna and Buna were both broken into pieces and sold to different buyers, rather than being sold whole. What might a historian or an economist gain from studying the fragments separately, that they would miss by treating "Leuna" or "Buna" as if they were still single, unified companies after 1990?
  2. Hoechst no longer exists as an independent company; BASF still does. Using everything you have read across Parts 5, 6 and 7, suggest two or three factors that might explain why one 1952 successor survived under its own name and another did not. Which of your suggested factors do you feel most confident about, and which are more speculative?
  3. This reading calls its most recent material "not yet history," while treating the events of the 1990s as more settled. Where, roughly, do you think the line between "recent events" and "history" actually falls, and does it fall in the same place for every kind of event?
  4. Bayer's acquisition of Monsanto brought with it a wave of litigation the company had not itself created. What responsibilities, if any, do you think a company takes on for a previous company's actions when it buys that company outright? Does your answer change if the harm was already publicly known about before the purchase went through?
  5. Motorenfabrik Oberursel is the one site in this whole series that has not been broken up, merged away, or forced into closure. Why might a small, comparatively unglamorous engineering site have had an easier three decades than the vast chemical works this series has otherwise followed?
  6. Looking back across all seven parts of this series: pick one single decision, by one person or one company, that you think mattered more than any other to how this whole 225-year story turned out. Defend your choice, and explain what you think would have been different if that decision had gone the other way.
