Cheat sheet: the two theories

The Particle Theory

  • All matter is made of particles, too small to see.
  • The particles are always moving. In a gas they move fast in all directions, far apart. In a liquid they slide past each other. In a solid they vibrate in fixed positions.
  • The hotter the substance, the faster its particles move.
  • Gas pressure comes from particles hitting the walls of the container.

The Collision Theory

A reaction happens when particles collide (1), with at least the activation energy (2), and with the right orientation (3). A collision that meets all three conditions is a successful collision. The rate of reaction is the number of successful collisions each second.

the change → what it does to the particles → collisions each second → successful collisions each second → the rate

  • Particles: what does that do to the particles? (Particle Theory)
  • Collisions: how many collisions happen each second? Is a bigger fraction of them successful? (Collision Theory)
  • Rate: so the number of successful collisions each second goes up, and the rate goes up.

The Exploration

Evidence for particles: Brownian motion

Cheat sheet: Brownian motion
  • Brownian motion is the random zigzag movement of tiny visible specks (pollen in water, smoke in air).
  • Cause: invisible, fast-moving molecules keep hitting the speck. At any moment the hits on one side are not balanced by the hits on the other.
  • It is our evidence that particles exist and are moving all the time.
  • Robert Brown, London, 1827: saw it. Albert Einstein, 1905: explained it. Jean Perrin, Paris: measured it and confirmed the explanation.
  • Warmer: the molecules move faster, so the specks jiggle more.

Brownian motion
the random zigzag movement of visible specks, caused by collisions with invisible fast-moving particles.

In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown, working in London, watched pollen grains floating in water through a microscope. The grains jiggled in random directions and never stopped. He first wondered whether the pollen was alive, but dust from rocks that had never been alive did the same. In 1905 Albert Einstein showed that this is exactly what we would see if the grains were being hit by huge numbers of much smaller, invisible, fast-moving water particles, with slightly more hits from one side than the other at any moment. Jean Perrin, working in Paris, measured the movement of grains carefully and found numbers that matched Einstein's predictions. That result convinced almost every remaining sceptic that particles are real.

In class we use smoke in air instead of pollen in water. The smoke specks are big enough to see through a microscope, and the air molecules are not.

The Collision Theory in pictures

Cheat sheet: the Collision Theory

A collision is successful only if ALL THREE are true:

  1. the particles collide;
  2. they hit with at least the activation energy (fast enough: a long arrow);
  3. they have the right orientation (the reactive parts face each other: black spots together).
  • Rate of reaction = the number of successful collisions each second.
  • Two ways to speed up a reaction: more collisions each second, or a bigger fraction of the collisions being successful.
  • Most collisions are not successful.

Task 1: powder or chip? (surface area)

The questionExplain why crushing a 2 g marble chip to a powder makes the reaction with dilute 0.5 M hydrochloric acid so much faster than with the chip.
Cheat sheet: surface area
bigger surface area → more marble particles exposed to the acid → more collisions each second → more successful collisions each second → faster rate
  • Only particles on the surface of a solid can be hit by the acid. Particles inside are protected.
  • Crushing does not change the mass or the number of particles. It exposes more of them.
  • Halve the edge of a cube: the total surface area doubles (24, then 48, then 96 cm² for our cube).
  • The temperature is the same, so the particles have the same energy, and the fraction of collisions that are successful is the same. The collisions are more numerous, not stronger.

Cheat sheet: gas pressure
double the pressure (half the volume) → twice as many particles in each cm³ → each A meets B twice as often → twice as many collisions each second → about twice the rate

  • Squeezing the same sample into half the volume, at the same temperature, doubles the number of particles in each cm³.

  • The number of particles in the sample is the same. Only the space has changed.

A model explanation

In a heap, only the flour on the outside can touch the oxygen in the air, so few collisions happen each second and the burning is slow. In a dust cloud, every tiny grain is surrounded by air, so the surface area is huge and oxygen particles collide with many more flour particles each second. The reaction is much faster, and the energy is released so quickly that it explodes.
