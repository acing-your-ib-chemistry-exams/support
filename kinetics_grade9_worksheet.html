Safety

  • Wear eye protection. Dilute hydrochloric acid at 0.5 M and 1.0 M is low in hazard, but acid in an eye must be rinsed with water straight away: tell your teacher.
  • Marble powder: do not breathe the dust. Weigh it over a tray and keep the pot closed.
  • The warm bath for the 35 °C run is warm, not hot. Read the temperature from a thermometer, not your hand.
  • Never seal the flask. Carbon dioxide must escape along the delivery tube into the gas syringe, or pressure builds up.
  • The pressure ideas in this worksheet are thought experiments about gases in industry. We never pressurise glassware.

The Particle Theory
  • All matter is made of particles, too small to see.
  • The particles are always moving. In a gas they move fast in all directions, far apart. In a liquid they slide past each other. In a solid they vibrate in fixed positions.
  • The hotter the substance, the faster its particles move.
  • Gas pressure comes from particles hitting the walls of the container.
The Collision TheoryA reaction happens when particles collide (1), with at least the activation energy (2), and with the right orientation (3). A collision that meets all three conditions is a successful collision. The rate of reaction is the number of successful collisions each second.

The Exploration

Evidence for particles: Brownian motion

In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown, working in London, watched pollen grains floating in water through a microscope. The grains jiggled in random directions and never stopped. He first wondered whether the pollen was alive, but dust from rocks that had never been alive did the same. In 1905 Albert Einstein showed that this is exactly what we would see if the grains were being hit by huge numbers of much smaller, invisible, fast-moving water particles, with slightly more hits from one side than the other at any moment. Jean Perrin, working in Paris, measured the movement of grains carefully and found numbers that matched Einstein's predictions. That result convinced almost every remaining sceptic that particles are real.

In class we use smoke in air instead of pollen in water. The smoke specks are big enough to see through a microscope, and the air molecules are not.

The reaction we are explaining

Marble is almost pure calcium carbonate. It reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid, and we measure the carbon dioxide with a gas syringe.

calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide

CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

In the acid, the hydrogen ions, H+, are the particles that collide with the calcium carbonate.

A fair testEvery run uses 2 g of marble and 100 cm³ of acid at 25 °C unless we change that factor on purpose. We change one factor at a time. 100 cm³ of 0.5 M acid is more than enough to dissolve 2 g of marble, so the marble runs out first and every run makes about 480 cm³ of carbon dioxide.

Task 4: warmer acid

The questionExplain why acid at 35 °C gives a rate of reaction about twice as fast as acid at 25 °C.

Particle Theory first

kineticfasterslowermorefractionnumber

Warmer particles have more kinetic energy, so they move . Brownian motion supports this: in warmer water the pollen grains jiggle vigorously.

How many more collisions?

A rise of 10 °C makes the particles move only about 2 in every 100 faster. So the number of collisions each second rises by only about in every 100.

Could that small rise alone double the rate? So the rest of the doubling must come from a bigger of the collisions being successful.
