6.1 Collision theory and rates of reaction

Measuring rates of reaction experimentally

Video link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LstMh4V2PiA

Watch this first.

In the previous section, we learned that the rate of a reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. Therefore, experiments to measure the rate of reaction should measure the rate of increase or decrease in concentration of a reactant or product, either directly or indirectly. In this section, we will discuss suitable experiments that can be carried out in a high-school laboratory.

Exam tip

You should be aware of several different experimental techniques that can be used to collect data for measuring rates of reaction. 

Stage 1: What is our definition of rate?

about 11 min

Learning objectives

By the end of this stage, I can:

Cheat sheet for stage 1
Rate of reaction: the change in concentration of a reactant or product divided by the change in time.
rate of reaction = change in concentration, Δcchange in time, Δt

Concentration is measured in mol dm−3 and time in seconds, so the unit of rate is mol dm−3 s−1. A rate of 0.0050 mol dm−3 s−1 means that the concentration changes by 0.0050 mol dm−3 every second.

Reactants and products. A reactant is used up, so its concentration falls. A product is made, so its concentration rises. We always take Δc and Δt as positive sizes (the larger value take away the smaller value), so a rate is never negative.

Why a measurement. “Fast” and “slow” are opinions. A rate has a number and a unit, so two reactions, or two runs of one reaction, can be compared.

Worked example. A reactant falls from 0.90 mol dm−3 to 0.60 mol dm−3 in 45 s. Find the average rate.
change in concentration, Δc = 0.90 − 0.60 = 0.30 mol dm−3
rate of reaction = 0.3045 = 0.0067 mol dm−3 s−1

This is an average rate over 45 s. The rate changes as the reaction goes on; stage 8 shows how to find the rate at one moment.

We seldom measure concentration directly while a reaction is running. We measure something that changes in step with it, record it at regular times, and use the record to find the rate.

Choosing a property: ask these questions in order

  1. Is a gas made, and can it be collected or can it escape? Follow the volume, the pressure or the mass.
  2. Is there a coloured substance? Follow the colour with a colorimeter.
  3. Does something sharp happen, such as cloudiness or a sudden colour? Time a fixed change.
  4. Are ions or an acid or alkali used up or made? Follow the conductivity or the pH.
  5. Nothing else works? Take samples and titrate them.

A stand-in for concentration. Each property above rises or falls in step with the concentration of a reactant or product. Because it goes up and down together with the concentration, the way it changes with time tells us how the rate changes.

Reading: gas syringe and balance

Measuring the production of a gas 

Common reactions that produce gases are the reactions of metals and metal carbonates with acids such as hydrochloric acid (see section 8.2.1). Figure 1 shows a gas syringe being used to collect the carbon dioxide produced in the reaction between calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid. The volume of gas produced is recorded at set time intervals and the results are plotted on a graph. The reaction between calcium carbonate and hydrochloric acid, for example, produces carbon dioxide gas according to the following equation:

CaCO3 (s) + 2HCl (aq) → CaCl2 (aq) + H2O (l) + CO2 (g)

 

An example graph is shown in Figure 2. The volume of carbon dioxide is plotted on the y-axis, against time on the x-axis. Note that two curves have been plotted on the same axes, for comparison. The curve labelled I has a steeper gradient than the curve labelled II; this curve (I) was produced using an acid of higher concentration. As we will learn in section 6.1.6, an increase in concentration increases the rate of reaction.

 

The rate of production of a gas can also be measured using a mass balance, as shown in Figure 3. In this experiment, the change in mass per unit time is measured as the carbon dioxide escapes from the conical flask. (Note that the cotton wool is used to prevent any acid spraying from the flask; it does not prevent the gas from escaping.)

 

 

An example graph for this experiment is shown in Figure 4. Again, two curves have been plotted on the same axes, one for larger pieces of calcium carbonate and one for smaller pieces. This experiment can be used to demonstrate the effect of surface area of reactants on the rate of reaction.

 

Explore: the gas syringe

Change the concentration of the acid and watch the plunger

A small mass of calcium carbonate (marble chips) reacts with 40 cm3 of hydrochloric acid, which is in excess, so the same total volume of carbon dioxide forms every time. Choose the concentration of the acid, then press Start. The plunger is pushed out by the gas, and the graph of volume against time draws itself. Each finished run stays on the graph so that you can compare runs.

Time: 0 sVolume of gas: 0.0 cm3Rate at this moment: 0.0 cm3 s−1

Think about it. Write your own answer first, then open the box to compare. The marked questions that use this explorer are 3.7, 3.8 and 3.9 in stage 3.

1. Run 0.2 mol dm−3 and then 1.0 mol dm−3. What is different about the two curves, and what is the same?

The curve for 1.0 mol dm−3 is much steeper at the start, so the plunger moves out faster and the reaction is finished sooner. Both curves level off at the same volume, because the same mass of calcium carbonate is used up each time.

2. Switch on the tangent. Run 0.4 mol dm−3 and then 0.8 mol dm−3. How do the two initial rates compare?

The initial rate at 0.8 mol dm−3 is twice the initial rate at 0.4 mol dm−3. When the concentration of the acid doubles, there are twice as many acid particles in the same volume, so collisions are twice as frequent and the rate doubles.

3. For one run, why does the curve become flatter as time goes on?

The calcium carbonate is being used up, so there is less of it to react and collisions with its surface become less frequent. The rate falls, and the curve flattens until all of the calcium carbonate has reacted and no more gas is made.

Set the reaction going in a flask, close the flask with a bung and a delivery tube, start the stopclock, and read the volume of gas at regular times, for example every 10 s.

What a gas volume tells us. The more product that is made, the more gas there is. At room temperature and pressure, one mole of any gas takes up 24000 cm3.

number of moles of gas, n = volume of gas, V (in cm3)24000
number of moles of gas, n = 4824000 = 0.0020 mol

Rate of gas production. We use the same idea as our definition, with volume of gas in place of concentration.

rate of gas production = change in volume of gaschange in time

The unit is cm3 s−1. The volume of the mixture is the same in every run, so the volume of gas made each second is proportional to the rate of reaction. It is a stand-in for the rate, and its unit is not mol dm−3 s−1.

Worked example. Magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid and hydrogen is collected in a gas syringe.
Time (s)0102030405060
Volume of gas (cm3)0132432384347

Average rate from 0 s to 20 s. The volume changes from 0 to 24 cm3, so the change in volume is 24 cm3.

rate = 2420 = 1.2 cm3 s−1

Average rate from 40 s to 60 s. The change in volume is 47 − 38 = 9 cm3.

rate = 920 = 0.45 cm3 s−1

The rate falls as the reaction goes on, because the acid is being used up and its concentration is falling.

Stage 4: How do we follow a reaction by the mass it loses?

about 11 min

Learning objectives

Cheat sheet for stage 4

Marble chips (calcium carbonate) react with hydrochloric acid and carbon dioxide escapes. We stand the flask on a balance, plug the neck with cotton wool, and read the mass at regular times.

  • The cotton wool lets gas out but stops drops of liquid being carried out with it, because lost drops would also lose mass.
  • The gas must be heavy enough to change the reading. Carbon dioxide (molar mass 44) loses a few tenths of a gram. Hydrogen (molar mass 2) loses so little that a balance cannot show it clearly.
mass of gas lost = starting mass − mass at that time
rate of mass loss = change in mass of gas lostchange in time

The unit is g s−1. As with gas volume, the mass of gas lost is proportional to the amount of product made, so the rate of mass loss is a stand-in for the rate of reaction.

Stage 5: How do we follow a reaction by its colour?

about 12 min

Learning objectives

By the end of this stage, I can:

Cheat sheet for stage 5

A coloured solution takes light out of a beam passing through it. A colorimeter measures how much light is taken out, and shows it as the absorbance. The more concentrated the coloured solution, the larger the absorbance.

  • Choose a filter that the coloured solution absorbs strongly. A blue solution absorbs red and orange light, so we use a red or orange filter.
  • Set the meter to zero with a cuvette of water, and use the same cuvette for every reading.
  • Read the absorbance at regular times as the colour fades or grows.

From absorbance to concentration. We measure the absorbance of solutions of known concentration and plot a calibration graph. For a dilute solution it is a straight line through the origin.

For the line in Figure 6, the absorbance is 10 times the concentration. So we find the concentration from an absorbance like this:

concentration, c = absorbance10
Worked example. Blue copper(II) sulfate solution fades as zinc is added. The absorbance falls.

Reading: timing a precipitate

Measuring the time taken for the formation of a precipitate

The reaction between hydrochloric acid and sodium thiosulfate is an example of a clock reaction in which the time taken for a reaction to reach a certain point is measured. In this reaction, it is the formation of a precipitate of yellow sulfur which obscures a cross drawn under the reaction mixture (Figure 6). By varying either the concentration or temperature of the acid or sodium thiosulfate solution, the effect of the change can be measured by recording the time taken for the cross to be obscured.

Na2S2O3 (aq) + 2HCl (aq) → 2NaCl (aq) + SO2 (g) + H2O (l) + S (s)

 

The graph in Figure 7 shows the time taken for the cross to be obscured at different temperatures. From the graph, it can be seen that the time it takes for the cross to be obscured decreases as the temperature of the solution increases. The effect of temperature on the rate of reaction is discussed in more detail in section 6.1.6.

Stage 6: How do we follow a reaction by timing a fixed change?

about 9 min

Learning objectives

By the end of this stage, I can:

Cheat sheet for stage 6

Instead of measuring how much change happens in a time, we fix the change and measure the time it takes.

  • Disappearing cross. Sodium thiosulfate reacts with hydrochloric acid and solid sulfur forms. We time how long the cloudiness takes to hide a cross, or the rulings on graph paper, viewed from above.
  • Iodine clock. A fixed amount of a second reactant is in the mixture. When it is used up, a colour appears suddenly. We time how long that takes.

Each run needs the same change, so the same amount of sulfur must form to hide the cross, or the same amount of the second reactant must be used up. So Δc is the same every time, and the rate is proportional to 1 over the time taken.

stand-in for rate = 1time taken, t

The unit is s−1. It shows how the rate compares from run to run. It is not a rate in mol dm−3 s−1.

Worked example. Run A takes 25 s and run B takes 50 s.
run A = 125 = 0.040 s−1
run B = 150 = 0.020 s−1

Run A has twice the stand-in for rate, so it is twice as fast. Half the time means twice the rate.

Reading: ions and pH

Measuring the change in ion concentration

For a solution to conduct electricity, it must contain free-moving ions. Where a reaction involves ions, we can determine the rate of reaction by using a conductivity meter to measure the change in ion concentration of an aqueous solution. Reactions that involve aqueous ions include acid–base reactions (which involve changes in hydrogen ion (H+) concentration). The increase or decrease in hydrogen ion concentration can be measured using a pH probe (Figure 5).

Reading: sampling and titration

Measuring the change in concentration by titration 

The final method we will look at is quenching (or stopping) a reaction. This involves removing a sample of the reaction mixture and quenching the reaction by placing it in cold water (or ice). We can then determine the concentration of either reactant or product by titrating with a standard solution (Figure 8).

 

An example of a reaction that can be analysed by redox titration is the one between iodine and propanone. These substances react in the presence of a sulfuric acid catalyst according to the following equation:

CH3COCH3 (aq) + I2 (aq) → CH3COCH2I (aq) + HI (aq)

Samples of the reaction mixture are removed at regular time intervals and quenched with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution. This removes the acid catalyst, quenching the reaction. The amount of iodine remaining can then be determined by titration with sodium thiosulfate solution, with a starch indicator.

Stage 7: What other ways are there?

about 7 min

Learning objectives

By the end of this stage, I can:

Cheat sheet for stage 7
PropertyWhat it measuresExample: hydrochloric acid + magnesium oxide
conductivityhow well the solution carries an electric current, which depends on the ions in ithydrogen ions are very good carriers. They are used up and replaced by magnesium ions, so the conductivity falls.
pHhow acidic or alkaline the solution isthe acid is used up, so the pH rises.

A probe connected to a data logger, such as Logger Pro, records a reading every second or two and draws the graph as the reaction goes on. Readings can be changed into concentrations with a calibration, as with the colorimeter. The pH scale is not proportional to concentration, so pH readings must be changed into concentrations before we find a rate.

Samples and titration

  1. Mix the reactants and start the stopclock.
  2. At set times, take a measured sample with a pipette.
  3. Quench the sample: stop the reaction at once, for example by running it into ice-cold water.
  4. Titrate the quenched sample to find its concentration.
  5. Plot concentration against time.

This method gives the concentration itself, so Δc over Δt applies without a stand-in. Each titration takes time, so it is best for slow reactions that change little while a sample is being titrated.

Stage 8: How do we turn the readings into a rate?

about 12 min

Learning objectives

By the end of this stage, I can:

Cheat sheet for stage 8

Plotting the graph

  1. Put time on the horizontal axis and the measured property on the vertical axis. Label each axis with the quantity and its unit.
  2. Choose a scale that lets the points fill the graph paper.
  3. Plot each point accurately.
  4. Draw one smooth curve through the points. Do not join the points with straight lines.

Why the curve flattens. As the reaction goes on, the reactant is used up. There are fewer particles in the same volume, collisions are less frequent, and the rate falls.

RateHow we find itWhat it tells us
average rateread two points on the curve; the change on the vertical axis over the change in timethe rate over a stretch of time
rate at one momentdraw a tangent to the curve at that time; the rise over the run of the tangentthe rate at that time
initial ratedraw the tangent at 0 sthe rate when every reactant is at its full concentration
Worked example. Average rate from 20 s to 60 s in Figure 7. The curve reads 24 cm3 at 20 s and 47 cm3 at 60 s, so the change in volume is 23 cm3 and the change in time is 40 s.
average rate = 2340 = 0.58 cm3 s−1

Initial rate from the tangent at 0 s. The tangent rises 60 cm3 over a run of 40 s.

initial rate = 6040 = 1.5 cm3 s−1

The initial rate is greater than the average rate, because the curve is steepest at the start.

Drawing a tangent

  1. Place a ruler so that it touches the curve at one point only, with the curve the same angle either side of the point.
  2. Draw the line long, so the triangle can be large.
  3. Draw a right-angled triangle on the line, as large as you can.
  4. Read the rise (the change on the vertical axis) and the run (the change in time), both as positive sizes.
rate at that moment = rise, the change on the vertical axisrun, the change in time
Worked example: a rate in mol dm−3 s−1. In Figure 8 the tangent at 30 s has a rise of 0.76 mol dm−3 and a run of 120 s.
rate at 30 s = 0.76120 = 0.0063 mol dm−3 s−1

Why use the initial rate. At 0 s every reactant is at its full concentration and no product has built up. Initial rates from different runs then compare like with like, for example when we change the concentration or the temperature.
