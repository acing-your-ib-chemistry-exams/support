Industrial Chemistry · Unit 2 · Background reading · Draft

Part 6 Two Chemistries, One Country: 1951–1990 The same firms, the same sites, the same chemistry — rebuilt as rival economic systems either side of one border, until the border itself came down

Part 5 ended in 1951–52, with IG Farben formally broken back into BASF, Bayer, Hoechst and smaller successors including Agfa. This reading follows what those pieces, and the sites this series has already visited — Ludwigshafen, Leverkusen, Frankfurt, Leuna, the Buna works, and Motorenfabrik Oberursel in the Taunus — did with the next thirty-nine years. It is really two stories running side by side on either side of one internal German border: a private, increasingly global chemical industry rebuilding itself in the West, and the same industry's confiscated sites in the East, rebuilt instead as instruments of a planned economy. Both call themselves the continuation of German chemistry. This reading does not choose between them; it asks what each one actually became.

Part 6 · draft 1951 – 1990 Ludwigshafen · Leverkusen · Frankfurt · Leuna · Schkopau · Oberursel

This reading is built around a single, almost accidental symmetry: the border that split Germany in 1949 ran straight through the middle of the industry this whole series has been following. BASF's Ludwigshafen and Bayer's Leverkusen ended up in the West; the Leuna works and the Buna works — the very site Part 5 spent so long on — ended up in the East, seized outright by the Soviet occupying power before the Cold War border even properly hardened. Both halves called what they were doing the future of German chemistry. This reading follows both, on purpose, rather than picking the more comfortable Western story and letting the other one drop out of the series.

Three threads run through it. First, the West German firms' reconstruction into private, increasingly global companies through the years West Germans themselves came to call the Wirtschaftswunder — the “economic miracle.” Second, Leuna and Buna's very different path: nationalised, renamed, and run for four decades as flagship enterprises of a planned economy, right up to the point where that economy could no longer afford the plan. Third, the much smaller Taunus thread: what happened to Motorenfabrik Oberursel once the Allies decided, then reconsidered, what a defeated Germany was allowed to build.

A note on this reading, before you start

This reading covers the German Democratic Republic's chemical industry as a real historical subject, not as a punchline. Life and work at Leuna and Buna under state planning is described here through what workers themselves recorded and later recalled, not through Western Cold War caricature. That does not mean the reading avoids the industry's genuine failures — chronic pollution, worn-out plant, an economy that could not keep its own promises — only that it tries to describe them as the people who lived through them did, rather than as a foregone conclusion.

Hold on to this, before either story gets underway. In the summer of 1950, Fritz ter Meer — sentenced at Nuremberg to seven years for his role in the Monowitz decision — was released early for “good behaviour.” In 1956, he was elected chairman of Bayer AG's supervisory board, a position he held for eight years.

Part one · 1948–1963

Currency, tax cuts, and a company that had 800 workers left

The West German recovery did not begin with chemistry at all. It began with money. On 20 June 1948, the Western occupying powers replaced the old, worthless Reichsmark with a new currency, the Deutsche MarkCurrency reform, 1948 — the Reichsmark, worthless after years of wartime and postwar inflation, was replaced overnight by the Deutsche Mark on 20 June 1948, instantly ending the shortage economy and giving West Germans a currency worth holding and saving., overnight ending years of rampant inflation and the barter economy that had grown up around a currency nobody trusted. The economics minister Ludwig Erhard paired the reform with the near-total abolition of wartime price controls and steep cuts to income tax — a resident earning 2,400 Deutschmarks a year in 1950 saw their marginal tax rate fall from 85% to 18%. Within a decade, journalists were calling the result the WirtschaftswunderWirtschaftswunder (“economic miracle”) — the rapid reconstruction and sustained growth of the West German economy from the 1948 currency reform through the 1960s and 70s. — the “economic miracle.”

BASF's own figures for these years are a useful measure of how far the chemical industry had fallen, and how fast it climbed back. Reincorporated as an independent company in 1952 after five years under Allied supervision, BASF employed only around 800 workers in the late 1940s; by 1963 that had grown to 45,000. Sales of nitrogen and ammonia products — the Haber–Bosch chemistry from Part 3, still the company's oldest business — were already approaching their wartime peak by 1957. None of the three successor companies started from nothing, exactly: they inherited land, plant and, crucially, chemists and engineers who had spent the 1930s and 40s at the same benches under IG Farben. What they had lost was patents, trade secrets seized as reparations, and a great deal of bombed or dismantled equipment; roughly 55% of BASF's surviving buildings in the late 1940s still held outdated machinery. Rebuilding meant modernising as much as reconstructing.

A concrete product, not just a statistic

Hoechst's 1953 polyester rights turned, within a couple of years, into a trade name still recognisable today: Trevira, a synthetic fibre used in everything from clothing to curtains, with a dedicated production plant built in Austria by 1965. It is exactly the kind of unglamorous, everyday consumer chemistry — alongside plastics, synthetic fertiliser and dyestuffs — that actually paid for the Wirtschaftswunder, far more than any single dramatic invention did.

Part two · 1945–1959

The other half of IG Farben becomes “the people's own”

Leuna and the Buna works, sitting in the Soviet occupation zone, never went through anything resembling BASF's 1952 reincorporation. The Soviet authorities seized both sites outright and, in the years immediately after the war, ran them as Soviet Joint-Stock CompaniesSoviet Joint-Stock Company (SAG) — a form of Soviet-controlled enterprise in the immediate postwar occupation zone, with output and profits flowing directly to the USSR, used at Leuna and Buna before their 1954 transfer to East German state ownership. — enterprises whose output and profits went directly to the USSR, a form of reparations by production rather than by dismantling machinery outright (though plenty of dismantling happened too: one former Leuna engineer later recalled that when the retreating Soviet forces first arrived, “they took everything,” including the technical blueprints and site records staff needed decades afterward to even map their own plant). This arrangement lasted until the 1953 workers' uprising across East Germany, after which the sites were formally transferred to East German state ownership on 1 January 1954.

Leuna was renamed VEB Leuna-Werke “Walter Ulbricht”VEB — Volkseigener Betrieb, “people's own enterprise”: the standard East German designation for a state-owned company. Leuna's plant was named after Walter Ulbricht, the SED's leader. — a VEBVEB — Volkseigener Betrieb, “people's own enterprise”: the East German designation for a state-owned company., or “people's own enterprise,” named after the East German Communist Party's own leader; Buna became VEB Chemische Werke Buna, Schkopau. In 1958, at a party conference remembered ever since for its slogan, Ulbricht himself announced the Chemieprogramm: “Chemie gibt Brot, Wohlstand und Schönheit” — “chemistry gives bread, prosperity and beauty.” The plan aimed to use Leuna's and Buna's existing chemical expertise to manufacture export goods that could earn hard Western currency, while also supplying East German consumers with the plastics-based household goods — egg cups, kitchenware, everyday plastic fittings — that made a modest kind of material comfort affordable under a planned economy. Construction began the following year, 1959, on a new petrochemical complex, Leuna II, designed to process crude oil piped in from the Soviet Union.

As historians, be on your guard! — two very different kinds of “rebuilt”

It would be easy to read the West's reconstruction and the East's Chemieprogramm as simply “capitalism succeeding, communism failing,” and there is real truth underneath that framing — the numbers later in this reading bear it out. But in 1958 that outcome was not yet obvious, and the Chemieprogramm was not cynical propaganda on its own terms: real chemists and engineers, many of whom had worked at Leuna and Buna since the IG Farben years, believed they were building something that could genuinely deliver bread, prosperity and beauty to people who had very recently had none of the three. Judge the outcome by what actually happened over the following three decades, not by assuming from 1958 how the story had to end.

Part three · 1960s–1980s

From national champions to global companies

Through the 1960s and 70s, BASF, Bayer and Hoechst grew from rebuilt national firms into genuinely global chemical multinationals, helped by the same conditions driving West Germany's wider boom: near-full employment (unemployment fell to just 0.7–0.8% in 1961–66 and again in 1970–71), rising wages (purchasing power rose 73% between 1950 and 1960 alone), and a large, skilled industrial workforce. BASF's biggest single move came in 1969, when it purchased Wintershall, then holder of half of the West German potash market and a quarter of its natural gas production — at the time, the largest acquisition in German corporate history, and enough to push BASF past Bayer into the position of the country's second-largest chemical company. Hoechst, meanwhile, built on its polyester success with steady diversification into petrochemicals and, by the 1970s, an increasingly important pharmaceutical business, reporting compounded growth of over 15% a year through the 1960s.

None of this happened in a vacuum, and it is worth being honest about what the figures leave out. These were also the decades in which West Germany's own reckoning with its recent past — what some historians call Vergangenheitsbewältigung, “working through the past” — was only just beginning in earnest, decades after the Nuremberg trials themselves. Fritz ter Meer's 1956 chairmanship, mentioned above, was not an isolated case of a convicted war criminal quietly returning to a boardroom; it was one visible example of a much wider, slower, and still-debated pattern across West German industry.

Part four · 1960s–1980s

The plan runs into a decade it had not budgeted for

Leuna II and the wider Chemieprogramm depended on one thing above all: cheap, reliable Soviet oil, delivered at preferential “friendship prices” well below the world market rate. The oil shocks of the 1970s and early 1980s took that arrangement apart. As global oil prices spiked and Soviet supply became less generous and less predictable, East German planners faced a choice between falling behind technologically or importing expensive Western equipment and know-how — and increasingly chose the latter, at costs the plan had never anticipated and hard-currency trade through ComeconComecon (Council for Mutual Economic Assistance) — the Soviet-led economic organisation coordinating trade and planning among Eastern Bloc states, including East Germany, from 1949 to 1991. could not fully cover. The anticipated gains from decades of investment in Leuna and Buna were, in large part, quietly eaten up by an energy crisis the plan had no mechanism to absorb.

The human and environmental cost accumulated alongside the economic one. Decades of coal-based chemical production with minimal pollution control, concentrated around Leuna, Buna and the nearby town of Bitterfeld, left the region — by the 1980s often described as among the most polluted industrial landscapes in Europe — with contaminated air, water and soil that outlived the state that created it. Workers still describe real pride in what they built and produced; the historical record also shows a plant that was, by the 1980s, ageing, under-invested, and increasingly unable to compete on either cost or environmental terms with its Western counterparts across the border.

A number worth sitting with

By the late 1980s, the Leuna and Buna complexes together still employed tens of thousands of people — entire towns built their identity and their working lives around the plants, exactly as Ludwigshafen and Leverkusen had in the West. Whatever judgement this reading reaches about the planned economy that ran them, it should not erase the fact that real people spent real careers there, made real things, and took real pride in doing so.

Meanwhile, in the Taunus hills · 1945–1990

An engine works waits out a ban, then finds a very different owner

Part 5 left Motorenfabrik Oberursel having produced a modest run of tracked artillery tractors and a handful of experimental engines that never reached service. At the war's end the factory was occupied by American troops, and for a short period in 1946 it was used as a US Army repair depot for tanks and trucks — a strange, quiet coda to a plant that had spent the war on aero-engine research. What followed was a decade of near-total dormancy, consistent with the Allied powers' postwar restrictions on German aircraft engine manufacture: the site sat effectively outside German industrial control until 1956, when it was finally returned to its prewar owner, Klöckner-Humboldt-DeutzKlöckner-Humboldt-Deutz (KHD) — the engineering group that had owned Motorenfabrik Oberursel since 1921 (see Part 4), and which resumed control of the site once postwar Allied restrictions eased in 1956. (KHD), and set to work on gas turbine development and production — a technical field with no wartime aircraft-engine baggage attached to it.

The site continued as a turbine works for the rest of this reading's period, renamed KHD Luftfahrttechnik GmbH in 1980. Then, right at the close of this reading's timeframe, in 1990 — the same year as German reunification itself — KHD sold the company to what was then a joint venture between BMW and Rolls-Royce. Under its new ownership the Oberursel works began work on the Rolls-Royce BR700 engine family, which would go on to power aircraft including the Boeing 717 and the Gulfstream V. The site still operates today as part of Rolls-Royce Deutschland: a century-long, four-owner story running from Adler bicycles and stationary gas engines, through wartime aero-engine research and a decade of Allied silence, to a German-British aerospace joint venture, landing, by coincidence rather than design, in the very year the rest of this reading's two Germanys became one again.

Hold on to this. Nobody planned for the smallest site in this entire series — a bicycle-and-engine works in a Taunus market town most students will never have heard of before Part 3 — to end up changing hands in the exact same year the country itself was reunified. It did anyway. Sometimes the tidiest parallel in a piece of history is simply a coincidence, and it is still worth noticing.

Meanwhile, elsewhere

Britain's own giant has a harder decade too

Part 5 introduced Imperial Chemical IndustriesImperial Chemical Industries (ICI) — Britain's major chemical conglomerate, formed 1926, for much of its history the largest manufacturer in Britain. (ICI) as Britain's answer to IG Farben's 1925 merger. Through the postwar decades ICI followed a path with real similarities to its West German counterparts: it had developed its own polyester fibre, Terylene, as early as 1941 (Hoechst's Trevira, by contrast, followed in the 1950s), expanded heavily into pharmaceuticals — forming a dedicated ICI Pharmaceuticals division in 1957 — and remained, for most of this reading's period, the largest manufacturer in Britain by output. Growth was not without friction: in 1960, ICI appointed its first chairman recruited from outside the company, Paul Chambers, whose restructuring drive included a failed attempt to take over the textile firm Courtaulds in 1961–62, a public setback for a company more used to being the one doing the acquiring.

Why this reading keeps ICI brief

ICI's own postwar story runs on a broadly similar rhythm to BASF, Bayer and Hoechst's — reconstruction, diversification, and growth into a global conglomerate — without an East German mirror to complicate it, since Britain was never divided the way Germany was. It is included here only as a light comparison, to show that West Germany's chemical industry was not uniquely successful in the postwar decades, but was rebuilding alongside a similarly-sized rival doing much the same thing from a very different starting position.

The close · 1989–1990

The border that ran through this whole reading comes down

On 9 November 1989, the Berlin Wall opened. Formal reunification followed on 3 October 1990. For BASF, Bayer and Hoechst, reunification meant a sudden, complicated new question: what, if anything, to do with the Leuna and Buna works — sites that had, on paper, once been part of the same IG Farben these three companies had themselves been split from in 1952. For the tens of thousands of East German chemical workers at Leuna and Buna, it meant the sudden exposure of four decades of underinvestment to Western competition and Western environmental standards, almost overnight, with no transition plan built for a change of this scale. For Motorenfabrik Oberursel, in the same year, it meant nothing at all to do with reunification directly — only the coincidence of a new owner, arriving from the opposite direction, in the same twelve months.

This reading ends here, deliberately, without following any of these threads into what actually happened to Leuna, Buna, or the wider East German chemical industry once reunification's full economic weight landed on them. That is a harder, more contested story than reconstruction was, and it belongs on its own, in the next reading, rather than as a rushed epilogue to this one.

Interlude

What was left to discover, chemically, by 1990?

By 1951, all ninety-two natural elements had names. Everything found from here on had to be made, not mined or spotted in a spectrum: nuclear reactors and particle accelerators, not chemistry benches. Ninety-eight elements were known in 1951 (six of them already laboratory-made); by 1990, that had reached 109, the last three — bohrium, hassium and meitnerium — made at a laboratory in Darmstadt, West Germany, using a technique precise enough to identify a single new atom at a time. The 98 elements known by 1951

Ninety-two of these are the natural elements this whole series of tables has been tracking since 1800. The other six — neptunium, plutonium, americium, curium, berkelium and californium — do not occur on Earth in any usable quantity, and were instead made, one after another, at the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory in California between 1940 and 1950, largely by teams led by Glenn Seaborg. Plutonium, the second of these, stopped being a laboratory curiosity and became a matter of enormous consequence within five years of its discovery: it was the explosive core of the bomb dropped on Nagasaki in 1945, a use its own discoverers had not remotely anticipated when they first made trace quantities of it in a Berkeley cyclotron.

Click through to 1990 — the transfermium wars

From element 99 onward, every element on this table was made by smashing atomic nuclei together and hoping a heavier one stuck, and almost every one of them existed for only a tiny fraction of a second before decaying away. Einsteinium and fermium, elements 99 and 100, were not even made on purpose: American scientists found them, unexpectedly, in radioactive debris collected after the first hydrogen bomb test at Enewetak Atoll in the Pacific in November 1952, then kept the discovery classified for three years. Mendelevium (1955), nobelium (1958) and lawrencium (1961) all followed from Berkeley, led by Albert Ghiorso's team. From element 104 onward, though, Berkeley found itself in direct competition with the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research at Dubna in the Soviet Union, and for years afterward the two labs, and the two Cold War superpowers behind them, disputed which had actually been first to make elements 104 through 106, and even what to call them — a fight chemists later nicknamed the “transfermium wars”, not fully resolved by an international committee until the 1990s. Then, right at the end of this window, a third contender entered the race: in 1981, 1982 and 1984, a team at the GSI Helmholtz Centre in Darmstadt, West Germany, using a new and far more precise technique for fusing nuclei together one atom at a time, produced elements 107, 108 and 109 — bohrium, hassium and meitnerium — and would go on, in the years just after this reading closes, to make three more. Germany's chemical industry, the subject of this whole series of readings, had by 1990 given way almost entirely to Germany's nuclear physics as the place new elements came from.

Before the next reading

Questions to think about

  1. BASF went from around 800 workers to 45,000 in fifteen years, largely on the back of currency reform, tax cuts and the removal of price controls. How much of the Wirtschaftswunder do you think was owed to specific chemistry and engineering decisions, and how much to those wider economic conditions? Can the two really be separated?
  2. In 1958, Walter Ulbricht's Chemieprogramm was a genuine, popular vision, not (yet) an obvious failure. What does it take for historians, looking back, to judge a plan by its outcome without assuming that outcome was inevitable from the start?
  3. Fritz ter Meer went from a Nuremberg conviction in 1948 to Bayer's chairmanship in 1956. This reading has now shown you that fact twice, in two different readings. Why might a course keep returning to the same fact rather than mentioning it once and moving on?
  4. The Leuna/Buna region's pollution was, by the 1980s, among the worst in Europe, while workers there also describe genuine pride in what they built. Explain why a historian should hold both of these things as true at once, rather than treating one as propaganda and the other as the "real" story.
  5. Motorenfabrik Oberursel changed ownership in the same year as German reunification, for reasons that have nothing to do with reunification. What is the difference between a genuine historical connection and a coincidence that simply happens to line up? Why does that distinction matter when you are building an argument from evidence?
  6. This reading ends without telling you what happened to Leuna and Buna after 1990. Based on everything else in this reading, what do you predict happened to a heavily polluted, underinvested, formerly state-planned chemical works once it was suddenly exposed to a competitive Western market? What questions would you want answered before trusting your own prediction?
