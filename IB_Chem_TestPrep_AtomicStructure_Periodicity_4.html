Key definitions — flip to reveal

Click or tap a card (or press Enter/Space when focused) to flip it.

Periodicity
The repeating trends in the physical and chemical properties of elements when arranged in order of increasing atomic number — trends that repeat across each period and down each group.
Period
A horizontal row of the periodic table. The period number equals the highest principal energy level (n) occupied by electrons in a ground-state atom of that element.
Group
A vertical column of the periodic table. Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons and therefore similar chemical properties.
Block (s, p, d, f)
A region of the periodic table named after the subshell being filled by the last electron added, in order of increasing atomic number: s-block, p-block, d-block, f-block.
Valence electron
An electron in the outermost occupied principal energy level of an atom; these electrons are primarily responsible for chemical bonding and reactivity.
Effective nuclear charge
The net positive charge experienced by an electron in a multi-electron atom, after accounting for the shielding (repulsion) from other electrons between it and the nucleus.
Shielding
The reduction in the attractive force from the nucleus on outer electrons, caused by repulsion from electrons in inner, fully or partly occupied shells.
First ionization energy (IE₁)
The energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
Successive ionization energies
The sequence of energies needed to remove each electron from an atom one at a time (IE₁, IE₂, IE₃ …); each is larger than the last, with large jumps marking the boundaries between shells.
Atomic radius
A measure of the size of an atom, generally taken as half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded (or touching) atoms of the same element.
Metallic character
The tendency of an element to lose electrons and form positive ions (cations); it increases down a group and decreases across a period.
Non-metallic character
The tendency of an element to gain electrons and form negative ions (anions), or to share electrons covalently; it increases across a period and decreases down a group.
Basic oxide
An oxide that reacts with acids (but not bases) to form a salt and water; typical of metals on the left of a period, e.g. Na₂O, MgO.
Acidic oxide
An oxide that reacts with bases (but not acids) to form a salt and water; typical of non-metals on the right of a period, e.g. SO₃, Cl₂O₇.
Amphoteric oxide
An oxide that can react with both acids and bases, showing both basic and acidic behaviour; e.g. Al₂O₃, which sits at the metal/non-metal boundary of Period 3.
Oxidation state
A number assigned to an atom in a compound or ion that represents the charge it would have if all bonds to different atoms were treated as fully ionic — it tracks how many electrons are considered transferred in forming those bonds.
Electronegativity
A measure of the ability of an atom in a covalent bond to attract the shared pair of electrons toward itself.
Discontinuity (in first ionization energy)
A break in the otherwise increasing trend of first ionization energy across a period, caused by a change in sub-shell (e.g. Be→B) or by electron-electron repulsion in a doubly occupied orbital (e.g. N→O).
Principal quantum number (n)
The quantum number describing the main energy level (shell) an electron occupies; it determines the period number of the element whose outer electrons occupy that shell.
Reactivity series
An order of elements (typically metals or halogens) ranked by how readily they undergo their characteristic chemical reaction — losing electrons for metals, gaining electrons for halogens.

Cheat sheet
  • A very large jump between successive ionization energies marks a change of shell: removing the next electron means breaking into a full inner shell, which is held much more tightly.
  • Counting how many ionization energies occur before the first big jump tells you the number of valence (outer-shell) electrons — which equals the group number (for main groups 1–2 and 13–18).

Hint 1Work out the ratio between each successive pair of values (IE₂÷IE₁, IE₃÷IE₂, etc.) — the biggest jump by far is the one that marks a change of shell.
Hint 2Count how many ionization energies come before that big jump: that count equals the number of valence electrons — and hence the group.

S3.1.3 — Periodicity: general trends

“Periodicity refers to trends in properties of elements across a period and down a group.”

Question 5 SL
Cheat sheet
  • Across a period (left to right), nuclear charge increases while shielding stays roughly constant (electrons are added to the same shell) — this pulls electrons in more tightly.
  • As a result, atomic radius generally decreases across a period, while ionization energy and electronegativity generally increase.

Which property generally decreases across Period 3, from Na to Ar?

Hint 1Three of these four properties increase across a period as the nuclear charge rises — only one shrinks.

Cheat sheet

  • This weakens the attraction the nucleus has for the outer electron, so first ionization energy generally decreases down a group.

Which property generally decreases down Group 17 (from F to I)?

Hint 1Atomic radius, shell count and nuclear charge all increase going down a group — only one of these four options shrinks.

  • Only one quantity keeps rising in both directions: atomic number, since it simply counts protons and always increases as you move to a heavier element.

Which quantity increases both across a period (left to right) and down a group?

Hint 1Atomic radius, ionization energy and electronegativity all reverse direction between 'across a period' and 'down a group' — one quantity never reverses.

(c) [HL] Explain the discontinuity in first ionization energy between phosphorus and sulfur. [2]

Mark scheme (award 1 mark per point unless shown otherwise):

  • Electron-electron repulsion between the paired electrons in that one 3p orbital in S makes one of them easier to remove, so IE₁ drops from P to S despite S's higher nuclear charge «1»
