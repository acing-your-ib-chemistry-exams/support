Enthalpy level diagrams and calorimetry: exothermic and endothermic changes

Links to NGSS HS-PS1-4: a graph of the relative energies of reactants and products is a model of the energy released or absorbed by a chemical reaction system.

Safety

The calculations below use results from two experiments. If you carry the experiments out yourself:

  • Wear eye protection throughout.
  • Copper(II) sulfate solution is harmful if swallowed and irritates the eyes. It is very toxic to aquatic life, so none goes down the sink: pour all of it into the labelled waste container.
  • Zinc powder is flammable as a dust. Keep it away from flames.
  • Ammonium nitrate is an oxidiser and harmful if swallowed. Keep it away from fuels and flames, and wash your hands after the experiment.
  • Stir with a stirring rod, never with the thermometer. Report any breakage at once.

Learning objectives and key ideas

By the end of this page you will be able to:

  • draw and read an enthalpy level diagram for an exothermic and an endothermic change;
  • link the sign of ΔH to a drop or a rise on the diagram, and explain the link with the system and the surroundings;
  • calculate ΔH from the temperature change in a calorimeter for an exothermic change and for an endothermic change.

Key ideas

  • ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants).
  • Exothermic: the system releases energy to the surroundings. The enthalpy of the system drops, ΔH is negative, and the surroundings warm up.
  • Endothermic: the system absorbs energy from the surroundings. The enthalpy of the system rises, ΔH is positive, and the surroundings cool down.
  • A thermometer in the solution measures the temperature. Temperature is the average kinetic energy of the particles (definition).
  • The energy gained or lost by the water is q = volume × c × ΔT, or simply q = vcΔT.
  • Learn: a temperature rise means an exothermic reaction, so ΔH is negative. A temperature fall means an endothermic reaction, so ΔH is positive.
  • ΔH = q ÷ n, in kJ mol−1, where n is the amount of the limiting reactant. The sign of ΔH is added at the end, from the rule above.
  • These diagrams show only the starting level and the finishing level. The route between them is for a later lesson.

The Exploration

Part A: enthalpy level diagrams

Cheat sheet: enthalpy level diagrams
  • The vertical axis is enthalpy. Draw one bar for the reactants and one bar for the products.
  • ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants). The arrow always runs from the reactants to the products.
  • Drop (products lower): ΔH is negative, the change is exothermic.
  • Rise (products higher): ΔH is positive, the change is endothermic.
  • The length of the arrow is the size of ΔH, in kJ mol−1.

To draw one: (1) draw the axis and the reactants bar. (2) Ask: does the system release energy or absorb it? (3) Put the products bar lower if it releases energy and higher if it absorbs energy. (4) Draw the arrow from the reactants to the products and label it with the sign and value of ΔH.

Common slip: the left-hand bar is not always the reactants. Work out which bar is which from the type of change.

An enthalpy level diagram has enthalpy on the vertical axis. A horizontal bar marks the enthalpy of the reactants and another marks the enthalpy of the products. The vertical gap between them is the size of the enthalpy change, ΔH, and the direction of the move from reactants to products gives its sign.

Part B: the sign of ΔH, the system and the surroundings

Cheat sheet: the sign of ΔH, the system and the surroundings
  • The system is the reacting chemicals. The surroundings are everything else: water, cup, thermometer, air.
  • A thermometer in the solution measures the surroundings. ΔH belongs to the system. They move in opposite directions.
  • Energy is conserved: energy lost by the system = energy gained by the surroundings, and the reverse.

Chemists divide the world into two parts. The system is the reacting chemicals. The surroundings are everything else that can exchange energy with them: the water, the cup, the thermometer and the air. A thermometer in the solution reads the temperature of the surroundings, not of the system. Energy is conserved, so energy that leaves the system arrives in the surroundings, and the reverse.

Part C: calorimetry with an exothermic reaction

Cheat sheet: calorimetry step by step
  1. v = volume of solution in cm3.
  2. ΔT = how far the temperature moved: the difference between the starting temperature and the highest (or lowest) temperature, in °C. Subtract the smaller from the larger.
  3. q = v × c × ΔT, with c = 4.18 J cm−3 °C−1. The answer is in J. Divide by 1000 for kJ.
  4. Learn: how to find the number of moles in a solution of known concentration when you know its volume in cm3.
    n = concentration × volume ÷ 1000
    Concentration in mol dm−3 (mol/litre) and volume in cm3 give n in mol. The ÷ 1000 changes cm3 into dm3 for you. For a solid, use n = mass ÷ molar mass.
  5. Limiting reactant: in a 1 : 1 reaction it is the one with the smaller n. Use its n in the next step.
  6. ΔH = q (in kJ) ÷ n, in kJ mol−1.
  7. Last, the sign. Learn: temperature rise → exothermic → ΔH negative. Temperature fall → endothermic → ΔH positive.

Worked example (different numbers). 80.0 cm3 of solution warms from 18.0 °C to 24.5 °C when 0.0150 mol of a solid reacts. ΔT = 24.5 − 18.0 = 6.5 °C. q = 80.0 × 4.18 × 6.5 = 2174 J = 2.17 kJ. ΔH = 2.17 ÷ 0.0150 = 145 kJ mol−1. The temperature rose, so the reaction is exothermic: ΔH = −145 kJ mol−1.

Why the measured size is too small: energy is lost to the air and absorbed by the cup and thermometer, so the highest reading is too low. Plotting temperature against time and extending the cooling line back to the time of mixing gives a larger, better ΔT.

Common slips: forgetting the ÷ 1000 in n = concentration × volume ÷ 1000; dividing by the amount of the reactant in excess; forgetting to change J to kJ; forgetting to put the sign on ΔH at the end.

Zinc powder is added to copper(II) sulfate solution in a polystyrene cup with a lid. Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s). The solution (the surroundings) is stirred and its temperature recorded. The limiting reactant sets the amount of reaction.

Part D: calorimetry with an endothermic change

Cheat sheet: endothermic calorimetry
  • The recipe is the same as in Part C: ΔT, then q = vcΔT, then ΔH = q (in kJ) ÷ n. The water is the surroundings and the dissolving solid is the system.
  • Learn: a temperature fall means an endothermic change, so ΔH is positive.
  • On the diagram the products sit above the reactants.
  • Heat leaks in from the warmer room, so the lowest reading is too high and the measured size is too small. Extending the warming line back to the time of mixing gives a larger ΔT.

Worked example (different numbers). 60.0 cm3 of water cools from 23.0 °C to 19.5 °C when 0.0200 mol of a solid dissolves. ΔT = 23.0 − 19.5 = 3.5 °C. q = 60.0 × 4.18 × 3.5 = 878 J = 0.878 kJ. ΔH = 0.878 ÷ 0.0200 = 43.9 kJ mol−1. The temperature fell, so the change is endothermic: ΔH = +43.9 kJ mol−1.

Common slip: giving ΔH a negative sign because the numbers on the thermometer went down. A temperature fall means endothermic, so ΔH is positive.

Solid ammonium nitrate is stirred into water in a polystyrene cup with a lid. NH4NO3(s) → NH4+(aq) + NO3−(aq). Here the dissolving solid is the system and the water is the surroundings.

Underlying ideas: definitions to learn

Calorimeter
An insulated container in which a change takes place so that the energy it transfers can be found from the temperature change of the contents. A polystyrene cup with a lid is a simple calorimeter.
Conservation of energy
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or converted. Energy lost by the system is gained by the surroundings, and the reverse.
Endothermic change
A change in which the system absorbs energy from the surroundings. The enthalpy of the system rises, ΔH is positive, and the surroundings get cooler.
Enthalpy (H)
The heat content of a system at constant pressure. The total enthalpy cannot be measured, but changes in enthalpy can.
Enthalpy change (ΔH)
H(products) − H(reactants). It is the energy released or absorbed by the system at constant pressure, in kJ per mole of reaction as written (kJ mol−1).
Enthalpy level diagram (energy profile)
A diagram with enthalpy on the vertical axis and a bar for the reactants and a bar for the products. A drop from reactants to products shows a negative ΔH, and a rise shows a positive ΔH.
Exothermic change
A change in which the system releases energy to the surroundings. The enthalpy of the system falls, ΔH is negative, and the surroundings get warmer.
Extrapolation
Extending a line beyond the measured points to estimate a value. Extending the cooling (or warming) line back to the time of mixing gives the temperature the mixture would have reached with no heat exchange with the room.
Limiting reactant
The reactant that is used up first. It sets how much reaction takes place, so ΔH per mole is calculated from the amount of this reactant.
Specific heat capacity (c)
The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). For water it is 4.18 J g−1 °C−1. One cm3 of water has a mass of 1 g, so it is also 4.18 J cm−3 °C−1, which is why q = vcΔT works with the volume in cm3.
Surroundings
Everything outside the system that can exchange energy with it: the solvent, the cup, the thermometer and the air. A thermometer in the solution measures the temperature of the surroundings.
System
The chemical reaction or change being studied: the reactants and products.
Temperature
The average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. A thermometer measures it.

Scale up: a cold pack

Cheat sheet: scaling up with ΔH
  • Amount: n = mass ÷ molar mass.
  • Energy absorbed or released: q = n × ΔH (size), in kJ.
  • Temperature change of a volume of water: ΔT = q ÷ (v × c), with q in J and v in cm3.

Worked example (different numbers). 20.0 g of a solid (M = 40.0 g mol−1) has ΔH = +12 kJ mol−1. n = 20.0 ÷ 40.0 = 0.500 mol. Energy absorbed = 0.500 × 12 = 6.0 kJ = 6000 J. In 150 cm3 of water: ΔT = 6000 ÷ (150 × 4.18) = 9.6 °C. ΔH is positive (endothermic), so the water cools.

A single-use cold pack contains 80.0 g of ammonium nitrate that dissolves in water when the pack is squeezed. Use the published value of ΔH = +25.7 kJ mol−1.
