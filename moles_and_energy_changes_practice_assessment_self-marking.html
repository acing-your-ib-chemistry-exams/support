System and surroundings

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. Magnesium ribbon is added to dilute hydrochloric acid in a test tube. The test tube becomes warm.

  1. The system is the magnesium and the hydrochloric acid, the chemicals that react.
  2. The surroundings are everything else: the water, the test tube, the thermometer and the air.
  3. The test tube gets warmer, so the surroundings gained energy. It came from the system.
  4. Surroundings hotter means exothermic, so ΔH is negative.
Method: what to do
  • The system is the part we study: the chemicals that react or change.
  • The surroundings are everything else: the water, the container, the thermometer and the air.
  • The thermometer is in the surroundings. A temperature rise means energy flowed from the system to the surroundings (exothermic, ΔH negative). A temperature fall means energy flowed from the surroundings to the system (endothermic, ΔH positive).
  • Energy is conserved: what the surroundings gain, the system loses, and the other way round.

Drawing enthalpy level diagrams

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. A reaction makes the surroundings hotter.

  1. The surroundings get hotter, so the reaction is exothermic.
  2. The system loses energy, so the products are lower than the reactants.
  3. A drop means ΔH is negative.

Exothermic: the products are lower.   Endothermic: the products are higher.

Method: what to do
  • An enthalpy level diagram has enthalpy on the vertical axis, a level for the reactants and a level for the products.
  • Exothermic: the products are lower (a drop). Energy is released to the surroundings, which get hotter. ΔH is negative.
  • Endothermic: the products are higher (a rise). Energy is absorbed from the surroundings, which get colder. ΔH is positive.

Reading enthalpy level diagrams

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. Reactants at 100 and products at 20 on the scale.

  1. Reactants: 100. Products: 20.
  2. 20 is lower than 100: a drop.
  3. Size of the change: 100 − 20 = 80.
  4. A drop means ΔH is negative, so ΔH = −80 kJ mol−1.
Method: what to do
  1. Read the level of the reactants and the level of the products from the scale (heavy numbers every 50, faint lines every 10).
  2. Lower products: a drop. Higher products: a rise.
  3. The size of ΔH is the bigger level minus the smaller level.
  4. Add the sign last: a drop means negative, a rise means positive.

Example: reactants at 100 and products at 20. The size is 100 − 20 = 80. A drop, so ΔH = −80 kJ mol−1.

Molar mass

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. The molar mass of CaCO3.

  1. Atoms: Ca 1, C 1, O 3.
  2. Ca: 1 × 40 = 40. C: 1 × 12 = 12. O: 3 × 16 = 48.
  3. Add: 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 g mol−1.
Method: what to do
  1. Write down the number of atoms of each element in the formula. A number outside a bracket multiplies everything inside it.
  2. For each element, multiply the number of atoms by its Ar (Table 1).
  3. Add all the masses together.
  4. Units: g mol−1.

Example: MgCl2 = 24 + (2 × 35.5) = 24 + 71 = 95 g mol−1

Moles to mass

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. The mass of 0.5 mol of CuO.

  1. Molar mass: 64 + 16 = 80 g mol−1.
  2. Estimate: 0.5 mol is less than one mole, so the mass is smaller than 80 g.
  3. m = n × M = 0.5 × 80 = 40 g.
Method: what to do
  1. Work out the molar mass, M, from Table 1.
  2. Estimate: more than one mole means the mass is bigger than M, and less than one mole means the mass is smaller than M.
  3. Use m = n × M.

Example: 0.5 mol of CaCO3: M = 100, so m = 0.5 × 100 = 50 g

Mass to moles

Core skill: secure this to build towards a 4.

Worked example

Example. The moles in 3.2 g of CH4.

  1. Molar mass: 12 + (4 × 1) = 16 g mol−1.
  2. Think carefully before calculating. Are you given more than one mole, or less than one mole just looking at the numbers given? 3.2 g is less than 16 g, so less than one mole.
  3. n = m ÷ M = 3.2 ÷ 16 = 0.2 mol. This is less than 1, as expected.
Method: what to do
  1. Work out the molar mass, M, from Table 1.
  2. Think carefully before calculating. Are you given more than one mole, or less than one mole just looking at the numbers given? If the mass is bigger than M you have more than one mole. If it is smaller than M you have less than one mole.
  3. Use n = m ÷ M, with the mass on the top.
  4. Check your answer against your decision in step 2.

Example: 11 g of CO2: M = 44. 11 is less than 44, so less than one mole. n = 11 ÷ 44 = 0.25 mol

Empirical formula from mass composition

Stretch: secure this to reach towards a 5.

Worked example

Example. 6.5 g of zinc reacts with 1.6 g of oxygen.

  1. Zn: 6.5 g is less than 65 g, so less than one mole. n = 6.5 ÷ 65 = 0.1 mol.
  2. O: 1.6 g is less than 16 g, so less than one mole. n = 1.6 ÷ 16 = 0.1 mol.
  3. Divide by the smallest (0.1): Zn 1, O 1.
  4. The empirical formula is ZnO.
Method: what to do
  1. For each element, think carefully before calculating: more than one mole or less than one mole? Then work out the moles: n = mass ÷ Ar.
  2. Divide every number of moles by the smallest number of moles.
  3. If the numbers are not all whole numbers, multiply them all by 2 (or 3, or 4) until they are.
  4. Write the formula using the whole numbers. Do not write the number 1.

Example: 5.6 g Fe and 2.4 g O: Fe 5.6 ÷ 56 = 0.1 and O 2.4 ÷ 16 = 0.15. Divide by 0.1: 1 and 1.5. Multiply by 2: 2 and 3. The formula is Fe2O3.

With percentages, imagine 100 g so that 80% becomes 80 g.

Energy transferred: q = v × 4.2 × Δt

Stretch: secure this to reach towards a 5.

Worked example

Example. 40 cm³ of one solution is mixed with 60 cm³ of another. The temperature rises from 20.0 °C to 24.0 °C.

  1. Total volume: 40 + 60 = 100 cm³. This is v.
  2. Δt = 24.0 − 20.0 = 4.0 °C.
  3. q = v × 4.2 × Δt = 100 × 4.2 × 4.0 = 1680 J.
  4. In kJ: 1680 ÷ 1000 = 1.68 kJ.
  5. The temperature rose, so the reaction is exothermic.
Method: what to do
  1. Use this equation:
    q = v × 4.2 × Δt
    The 4.2 is for water, and v is the volume in cm³ (1 cm³ of water has a mass of 1 g).
  2. If two solutions are mixed, v is the TOTAL volume: volume 1 + volume 2.
  3. Δt is the bigger temperature minus the smaller. There are no signs on q or Δt.
  4. The answer is in joules. Divide by 1000 to get kilojoules.
  5. Learn: a temperature rise means an exothermic reaction, and a temperature fall means an endothermic reaction.

Example: 50 cm³ of water rises by 6.0 °C: q = 50 × 4.2 × 6.0 = 1260 J = 1.26 kJ (exothermic)

Enthalpy change, ΔH, from a temperature change

Stretch: secure this to reach towards a 5.

Worked example

Example. 25 cm³ of a 0.50 mol dm−3 acid (the limiting reactant) is mixed with 25 cm³ of alkali. The temperature rises from 20.0 °C to 28.0 °C.

  1. Total volume: 25 + 25 = 50 cm³.
  2. Δt = 28.0 − 20.0 = 8.0 °C. q = 50 × 4.2 × 8.0 = 1680 J = 1.68 kJ.
  3. n of acid = 0.50 × 25 ÷ 1000 = 0.0125 mol (use the acid volume only; less than one mole, as expected).
  4. Size of ΔH = 1.68 ÷ 0.0125 = 134.4 kJ mol−1.
  5. The temperature rose: exothermic, so ΔH = −134.4 kJ mol−1.
Method: what to do
  1. Find v: for mixed solutions add the two volumes (the total); for a solid dissolving, use the volume of water.
  2. Work out Δt, then q in joules, then ÷ 1000 to get kJ:
    q = v × 4.2 × Δt
  3. Moles of the limiting reactant: n = concentration × volume ÷ 1000 for a solution (volume of that solution only, not the total), or n = m ÷ M for a mass. Think carefully before calculating: more than one mole or less than one mole?
  4. Size of ΔH = q (kJ) ÷ n, in kJ mol−1.
  5. Add the sign last: temperature rise, exothermic, ΔH negative. Temperature fall, endothermic, ΔH positive.

Underlying ideas: definitions to learn

Conservation of energy
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or converted. Energy lost by the system is gained by the surroundings, and the reverse.
Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element in a compound.
Endothermic change
A change in which the system absorbs energy from the surroundings. The enthalpy of the system rises, ΔH is positive, and the surroundings get cooler.
Enthalpy change (ΔH)
H(products) − H(reactants). It is the energy released or absorbed by the system at constant pressure, in kJ per mole of reaction as written (kJ mol−1).
Enthalpy level diagram (energy profile)
A diagram with enthalpy on the vertical axis and a bar for the reactants and a bar for the products. A drop from reactants to products shows a negative ΔH, and a rise shows a positive ΔH.
Exothermic change
A change in which the system releases energy to the surroundings. The enthalpy of the system falls, ΔH is negative, and the surroundings get warmer.
Limiting reactant
The reactant that is used up first. It sets how much reaction takes place, so ΔH per mole is calculated from the amount of this reactant.
Molar mass, M
The mass of one mole of a substance, in g mol−1. You find it by adding up the Ar values of all the atoms in the formula.
Mole (mol)
The unit for the amount of a substance. One mole contains 6.02 × 1023 particles, and one mole of a substance has a mass in grams equal to its relative formula mass.
Relative atomic mass, Ar
The mass of an atom of an element compared with other atoms. In this assessment we use whole numbers, except chlorine (35.5).
Surroundings
Everything outside the system that can exchange energy with it: the solvent, the cup, the thermometer and the air. A thermometer in the solution measures the temperature of the surroundings.
System
The chemical reaction or change being studied: the reactants and products.
